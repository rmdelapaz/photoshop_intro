Module 0: Your First Win

How to run Session 1: welcome the room, orient everyone to how this course works, get every copy of Photoshop working, and then deliver the single lesson that opens the course. Learners open a photo, straighten and crop it, brighten it with one adjustment layer, and export a share-ready JPEG, then do it again on their own photo. Protect the first-day energy: everyone leaves with a finished image.

1 lesson 3 hrs session Lesson L01 Level Absolute beginner Maps to Session 1

How to Use This Guide

  • The course runs as 8 sessions × 3 hours, one module per session. Session 1 = Module 0 (this guide), Session 2 = Module 1, and so on through Session 8 = Module 7 (Capstone).
  • Every lesson runs the same rhythm: Frame → Demo → Guided build → Solo variation → Check. The lessons are built this way on purpose (a guided build, then a "Now You" repeat with a twist), so follow their order.
  • Demo everything live on the projector. Beginners mirror your screen click for click. Say the menu path out loud every time ("File, Save As…"), and say both shortcuts: Ctrl on Windows, ⌘ on Mac.
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. "Say:" lines in the demo scripts are suggested wording; make them your own.
  • Quiz items live in the participant workbook's Self-check boxes; answers are summarized here and explained in full in the Answer Key.
The #1 risk of Session 1: install and sign-in drift

Photoshop is a Creative Cloud app. A learner who arrives with an expired trial, an unsigned-in account, or a half-finished download can lose the whole morning. Send a pre-class email (install Photoshop, sign in, launch it once, bring 4–5 of your own photos) and use the setup-check block to catch the rest. Never freeze the room to fix one laptop: pair that learner with a neighbor and keep moving.

Before Session 1

  • ☐ Every machine has Photoshop 2025 or later, signed in and launched once. (The course home page recommends the current version; the Remove tool used in Module 1 needs Photoshop 2023 v24.5 or later.)
  • ☐ Copy labs/module-0/practice/ to each machine (or a shared drive). These synthetic files are safe to redistribute. Do not hand out the course site's images/ folder: it is licensed stock and not redistributable.
  • ☐ Learners bring 4–5 photos of their own on the laptop, not only on a phone (see the list in labs/module-0/README.md). For today they need at least one that's a little dark or crooked and one that's too bright.
  • ☐ On the projector machine: reset the workspace (Window ▸ Workspace ▸ Reset Essentials) so your screen matches a fresh install, and raise the interface size if the room is large.
  • ☐ Open the course site in a browser tab: the home page (for orientation), recipes.html, and lesson_01.html (for the before/after slider).
  • ☐ A way to share results: a shared folder, a projector-connected laptop that can open learners' JPEGs, or a gallery walk around the room.

Course Orientation

20 min
Talking points & live-demo script
  1. Project-driven, not a tool tour (5m). Show the course home page. Say: "Most software courses march you through menus: here's the Brush tool, here's a dialog box you'll forget by lunch. This one doesn't. Every module ends in a finished deliverable, and you learn each skill at the moment the project needs it." Walk the path: Module 0 First Win, 1 Foundations (a messy photo cleaned up), 2 Light & Color, 3 Retouching, 4 Compositing, 5 Design & Type (a poster), 6 AI & Speed, 7 Capstone (a self-directed portfolio piece, presented). One module per session.
  2. It spirals (3m). Name the four big ideas that keep coming back, each time deeper: non-destructive editing, the layer stack, masks as grayscale, and selections. Say: "You don't finish layers today and move on. You'll keep meeting them until they're second nature."
  3. The Recipes library (4m). Open recipes.html. Type "horizon" in the filter box and show the "Straighten a crooked horizon" card. Say: "Recipes are short, standalone how-tos you can use in any order, each linked to the lesson that teaches the why. They're the reason you don't have to memorize everything." Every lesson also ends with its own recipe card; the workbook reprints them.
  4. Journaling (4m). Show the workbook's Journal box. Five things after every lesson: key concepts · techniques that clicked · questions or confusion · ideas to try · progress and feelings. Say: "Your first entry today is about why you're here. At the Capstone you'll read it again, and you'll be amazed how far you've come."
  5. Growth mindset (4m). Say: "If Photoshop feels like 'too much,' that feeling is normal, and temporary. You are not going to learn all of Photoshop. Nobody has. You're going to learn the handful of ideas it's built on, and then use them. Add the word yet to anything that feels hard today." Set the norm: mistakes are expected, and non-destructive editing means almost nothing you do in this course is permanent.

20 min
Sequence

Lesson Plan

L01

Fix & Export One Photo

50 min + 35 min independent
Objectives
  • Open a photo and straighten & crop it with the Crop tool
  • Improve its brightness and contrast with a Brightness/Contrast adjustment layer
  • Explain, in one sentence, what "non-destructive editing" means and why it matters
  • Export a finished JPEG without ever altering the original file
Timing (0:55–1:45)
4mThe 20-minute promise: show the lesson's before/after slider
8mOne idea: tracing paper (layers, adjustment layers, non-destructive)
30mGuided build, Steps 1–4 (demo a step, room does it, next step) + checklist
8mSelf-check (workbook Q1–Q6), answers aloud
Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. Open with the payoff (4m). Put lesson_01.html on the projector and drag the before/after handle. Say: "You don't need to understand everything today. You need to finish one photo. Understanding grows out of finishing, not the other way around."
  2. Tracing paper (8m). Hold a sheet of paper over a printed photo if you have one. Say: "Instead of drawing on the photo with a marker, we lay tracing paper over it. Brighter? One sheet. Warmer? Another sheet. Hate it? Peel it off. The photo underneath is never touched." Name the terms: those sheets are layers; sheets that only carry an instruction ("make everything under me brighter") are adjustment layers; working this way is non-destructive editing. Say: "Every professional retoucher works this way. It's not a beginner shortcut you'll outgrow; it's the real workflow, so you never have to unlearn a bad habit."
  3. Step 1: Open & save a working file (5m). Click File ▸ Open, choose l01-tilted-horizon.jpg. Immediately File ▸ Save As, format Photoshop (.psd), name myphoto-working.psd. If Photoshop asks where to save, click Save on your computer. Say: "The .psd remembers layers. The original JPEG stays untouched on disk. That's tracing paper applied to the file itself."
  4. Step 2: Straighten & crop (8m). Press C. Drag a corner inward to trim distracting edges (on the practice file, crop out the dark blob in the bottom-left corner). Then move the cursor just outside a corner until it becomes a bent arrow and drag to rotate until the grid lines match the horizon. Press Enter (Return). Say: "Straightening is just rotating the grid until it agrees with a line that should be level." Walk the room before moving on: this step spreads people out.
  5. Step 3: One adjustment layer (9m). At the bottom of the Layers panel click the half-black/half-white circle (◐) and choose Brightness/Contrast. Nudge Brightness up until the shadows open up; nudge Contrast up a little for punch. Point at the Layers panel: a new sheet sits above the photo. Then the magic moment: click the eyeball next to the adjustment layer off and on. Say: "The whole effect vanishes and comes back. Proof we changed nothing in the photo itself." Pause until every learner has clicked their own eyeball.
  6. Step 4: Save, then export (6m). File ▸ Save (Ctrl/⌘ + S) so the .psd keeps the adjustment layer. Then File ▸ Export ▸ Export As…, format JPG, quality around 80%, Export, name myphoto-final.jpg. Say: "Two files now: the layered .psd is your editable master, the flat .jpg is the copy you share. Never post the .psd. Never edit on the .jpg."
  7. Checklist (2m). Read the lesson's five-item Project Completion Checklist aloud; hands up for "all five." Say: "You just completed the entire Photoshop loop. Take the win."
  8. Self-check (8m). Learners answer workbook Q1–Q6 alone (4m), then you reveal answers and ask one volunteer to explain each "why."
Lab: Independent pass on your own photo (2:00–2:35)

This is the lesson's "Now You" solo variation, done on a learner's own photo. Repeat the whole loop without a demo. Suggested twist from the lesson: choose a photo that's too bright, lower Brightness a touch, then add a second adjustment layer (◐ ▸ Vibrance, push Vibrance up gently), and export. Compare side by side with the original. Anyone without a suitable photo uses l01-too-bright.jpg. Early finishers run the recipe on a third photo. Details in labs/module-0/README.md.

Your role: coach, don't drive. When someone asks "how do I…?", point to the recipe card in their workbook first. Name files with the learner's own subject (e.g. beach-working.psd, beach-final.jpg).

Circulate for: a working .psd saved before editing; two adjustment layers visible in the Layers panel (not one layer edited twice); an exported .jpg with a new name so the original isn't overwritten.

Definition of done: every learner has two finished loops (practice file + own photo), each with a .psd containing at least one adjustment layer and an exported .jpg, and can toggle the eyeball to show the before/after.

Share-out (2:35–2:50)

Each learner (or volunteers, in a large room) shows their own-photo .psd for 30–45 seconds: toggle the adjustment layers' eyeballs for the before/after and say one choice they made ("I lowered brightness because the sky was blown out"). Alternative: a gallery walk with laptops open to the .psd. Celebrate subtle edits out loud: the lesson's FAQ is clear that a subtle, tasteful edit done non-destructively is a complete success.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Skipping Save As .psd. Without a working .psd it's easy to save changes back over the original JPEG, a format that can't store layers, so the edit gets baked in for good. Make Save As the very first click, every time, and say why.
  • Saving to the cloud by accident. The first Save As may offer cloud documents. Tell the room to click Save on your computer so they can find the file afterward.
  • Crop tool remembers the last ratio. If the crop box snaps to a square or a fixed shape, open the ratio menu in the options bar and click Clear so the box drags freely.
  • Rotating by dragging inside the box. Dragging inside moves the image; rotating only happens with the bent arrow just outside a corner. Demo the cursor change slowly.
  • Forgetting to commit the crop. Nothing else works while the crop is pending. Press Enter/Return (or click the check mark in the options bar).
  • Pushing Brightness to the max. Say "nudge." A subtle edit is a complete success.
  • Export confusion. Export As writes a new file. If someone exports over their original (same name, same folder), they've lost it. Always use a -final name.
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) Changes live on layers above the photo, so the original pixels are never altered; Q2 B) A .psd keeps your layers, and the original stays untouched; Q3 A) Bent arrow just outside a corner, rotate to match the horizon; Q4 C) The edit lives on the adjustment layer, not in the photo; Q5 B) Save writes the layered .psd, Export makes a flat share-ready copy; Q6 A) Window ▸ Layers (or F7). Full explanations in the Answer Key.

First journal entry (2:50–2:57): Why do you want to learn Photoshop, and what's one image you dream of being able to make? Remind the room of the five journal headings and that you'll come back to this entry at the Capstone (Session 8). Paper learners use the workbook's Journal box.

Quick verbal check (during the share-out): "Explain non-destructive editing in one sentence." Listen for "the original is never changed."

Differentiation

Fast finishers: the lesson's "Going further": reorder the two adjustment layers by dragging one above the other and see whether the result changes. Then try the Straighten button in the Crop options bar (drag a line along what should be level) as a second way to straighten, and run the recipe on a third photo. Point them at the Recipes library's "Crop to an exact size or ratio" card.

Struggling learners: for the independent pass, drop the Vibrance twist and repeat Steps 1–4 exactly, with the workbook's recipe card beside them. Pair them with a finisher who "drives" only by pointing, never by taking the mouse. The only must-haves today are an exported JPEG and one eyeball toggle.

Closing the Session

Wrap-up (2:57–3:00)

Recap the loop on the board: Open → Save .psd → Crop → Adjust → Save → Export JPG. Golden rule: edits live on adjustment layers, never on the photo. Assign the lesson's "Before the next lesson": keep your .psd, run the 5-step recipe on two more photos until it feels automatic, finish your journal entry. Ask learners to bring photos for Session 2: a landscape with a pale sky, a photo with one clear subject (person, pet, or product), and a photo with dust, a blemish, or a distracting object. Preview Session 2 (Module 1): "Today's adjustment changed the whole photo. The best edits are local: brighten this, calm down that. Next time we meet the tool for 'only here, not there': the mask."

Lesson Plans

L02

The Layer Stack & Your First Mask

50 min
Objectives
  • Read a layer stack: what's on top wins, and why order matters
  • Explain a mask in one sentence: black hides, white reveals, gray partly reveals
  • Paint on a layer mask to apply an adjustment to only part of a photo
  • Recover from mistakes by painting the mask back: no undo panic required
Timing
3mThe problem: one edit, two regions (lesson slider)
4mMental model 1: the stack of glass
6mMental model 2: a mask is a stencil
3mReading the Layers panel: two thumbnails
20mGuided build: rescue the sky (Steps 1–4)
7mNow You: spotlight a subject
7mSelf-check, recipe card, journal
Teaching sequence & live-demo script
  1. The problem (3m). Show the L02 slider. Say: "To get detail in the land, the camera blew the sky out. Darken everything and the land goes muddy. We want to darken the sky only. Notice the hills are identical on both sides."
  2. Stack of glass (4m). Upgrade the Module 0 metaphor. Say: "Layers are sheets of glass in a frame, and you look straight down. Paint on a higher sheet covers what's below; clear glass lets the lower sheets show." Write the two rules: higher layers win where they're opaque and order is meaning.
  3. A mask is a stencil (6m). Every adjustment layer comes with a layer mask, a grayscale card that decides, pixel by pixel, how much of the effect gets through. Have the whole room say the sentence aloud: "Black hides. White reveals. Gray does a bit of both." Sketch a card that's white at the top, black at the bottom, gray between. Then the superpower: Say: "Painted over something by accident? Switch to the opposite color and paint it back. Nothing is ever destroyed. That's why pros mask instead of erase."
  4. Two thumbnails (3m). Point at an adjustment layer: the effect (◐) and the mask (white card). Say: "Before you paint, click the mask thumbnail so it gets a highlighted border. On an adjustment layer your brush lands on the mask anyway, but on a pixel layer with a mask, painting with the image thumbnail selected paints the photo itself. Build the habit now."
  5. Step 1 (2m). Open l02-washed-sky.jpg (or a learner landscape). File ▸ Save As ▸ sky-rescue-working.psd. Say: "You know the rhythm: protect the original first."
  6. Step 2 (3m). ◐ ▸ Brightness/Contrast. Pull Brightness down until the sky looks rich. Say: "Ignore the land going too dark. The mask will fix that. Right now the mask is all white, all revealed, so the darkening hits everything."
  7. Step 3 (8m). Click the mask thumbnail. Press D (default colors), then X so black is the foreground. Edit ▸ Fill…, Contents: Black, or press Alt/⌥ + Backspace/Delete. The darkening vanishes. Say: "Not Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace: that fills with the background color, which is white, so nothing happens." Press B, X for white, pick a big soft brush (] to enlarge), and paint over the sky. Say: "Watch the mask thumbnail: a white sky-shape is growing on the black card. That's your stencil." Deliberately paint onto a hill, then press X and paint it away.
  8. Step 4 (5m). Lower brush opacity to about 30% (with the Brush active, pressing 3 sets 30%) and gently blend along the horizon. Say: "This is painting gray: a partial reveal." Toggle the eyeball for the before/after, File ▸ Save, then File ▸ Export ▸ Export As ▸ JPG 80% → sky-rescue-final.jpg.
Lab: Rescue the Sky, then Now You

Guided build on l02-washed-sky.jpg; solo variation on l02-spotlight-object.jpg or a learner's portrait/object photo: brighten with a Brightness/Contrast layer, fill the mask black, paint white only over the subject; then a second, darkening layer masked to the background. Details in labs/module-1/README.md.

Circulate for: the mask thumbnail (not the photo) highlighted; a black mask with a white sky-shape in the thumbnail; a soft horizon, not a hard line.

Definition of done: the lesson's five-item checklist: one Brightness/Contrast layer above the photo, mask black over the land and white over the sky, a richer sky with untouched land, a soft transition, .psd saved and JPG exported.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Painting on the photo. If the Background (photo) layer is highlighted, the brush paints real white or black paint onto the picture. Symptom: white streaks in the sky. Fix: undo, click the mask thumbnail on the adjustment layer, paint again.
  • Fill did nothing. They used Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace (background color = white). Use Alt/⌥ + Backspace with black foreground, or Edit ▸ Fill ▸ Black.
  • Colors aren't pure black and white. A previous color pick left a tinted foreground, so the reveal is partial everywhere. Press D to reset.
  • Opacity left at 30%. After softening the seam, the next strokes barely reveal anything. Press 0 for 100% (with the Brush active).
  • Hard, obvious edge. The brush Hardness is high. Right-click the canvas and lower Hardness, then paint the seam at low opacity.
  • Undo panic. Learners hammer Ctrl/⌘ + Z after every slip. Redirect: "Press X and paint it back."
Check for understanding: self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) It covers what's below it; Q2 A) The adjustment is hidden there; Q3 C) Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace fills with the background color (white); Q4 B) Press X and paint black over the mistake; Q5 A) Click the mask thumbnail; Q6 C) Lower brush Hardness and paint the seam at low opacity. Full set in the Answer Key.

Journal (3 min, finish at home if needed): Describe, in your own words and without looking, what happens when you paint black on a mask versus white. If you can teach it to an imaginary friend, you own it. Pair-share option: learners explain it to a neighbor first, then write.

Differentiation

Fast finishers: the lesson's "Going further": select the mask thumbnail and press Ctrl/⌘ + I to invert it and watch the reveal flip. Then try the opposite workflow from the FAQ: start with a white mask and paint black to subtract, and decide which needs less painting for this shot. Alt/⌥-clicking the mask thumbnail shows the mask itself on the canvas (click again to return), a great way to inspect a seam.

Struggling learners: skip Step 4's seam softening at first; get a black mask with a white sky first. Hand them the recipe card and the three diagnosis questions. If the black/white logic won't stick, have them paint black and white stripes on the mask and toggle the eyeball: seeing the effect switch on and off stripe by stripe usually makes it click.

Selections That Hold Up

Teaching sequence & live-demo script

The Develop Step: Camera Raw

Objectives

Teaching sequence and demo script
  1. Frame: defuse the fear (2m). Say: "Curves is the one advanced control worth learning if you learn only one. It looks like math class. It isn't. It's a picture of 'make these tones brighter, those darker.' If it's confusing right now, add the word yet."
  2. Demo: read the graph on a ramp (5m). Open l06-tone-ramp.png (a black-to-white gradient with numbered steps). Click the ◐ button at the bottom of the Layers panel ▸ Curves. Say: "Every tone walks in along the bottom, its input, and walks out up the side, its output. The straight diagonal means every tone leaves exactly as it came in." Drag the middle point up: the whole ramp brightens. Drag it down: it darkens. Reset.
  3. Demo: the S-curve (4m). Switch to grade-working.psd (or l06-flat-landscape.jpg). Add Curves. Click about a quarter of the way up the line and drag gently down; click about three quarters up and drag gently up. Say: "Shadows down, highlights up. That's an S. The steeper the middle, the more contrast. Small moves, big results." Then show the opposite: lift the bottom-left corner for milky, faded blacks. Say: "That's the matte, vintage look."
  4. Demo: color-grade with a channel (4m). Change the channel dropdown from RGB to Blue. Lift the shadow end slightly (cool shadows), drop the highlight end slightly (warm highlights). Say: "On its own, Blue gives you blue and yellow. For a truer teal, switch to Red and pull its shadow end down slightly. Less red means more cyan." Warn: a few pixels of drag is already a strong grade.
  5. Demo: grade one region only (5m). Add a second Curves layer and brighten it noticeably. Click its mask thumbnail. Press D, then X so black is the foreground color, then Alt + Backspace / ⌥ + Delete to fill the mask black (or Ctrl/⌘ + I to invert the white mask). Press X for white and paint over the subject with a soft brush (B). Say: "That's the mask from Lesson 1.1, reused at a higher level. Beginner mistakes to pre-empt

  • Too many points. Two points make an S. Five points make a roller coaster. To delete a point, drag it off the graph.
  • Wild channel curves. "My colors went crazy." They moved the Blue or Red channel too far. Reset the channel and move a few pixels only.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt

  • Struggling learners: guarantee the two essentials: the crisp-vs-mushy test (for understanding) and the export (for the deliverable). The extra Smart Filter and filter mask are a bonus.
Check for understanding: workbook self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 C) A container that stores your original pixels; every edit is a re-editable instruction; Q2 B) It re-renders from the sealed original; Q3 A) Paint black on the Smart Filters mask over the skin; Q4 C) Double-click the filter's Blending Options icon, set Soft Light or Screen at 20–40%; Q5 B) Healing or cloning directly on the layer's pixels; Q6 A) The PSD is the re-editable master; the JPEG is flat. L08

Natural Skin: Heal, Don't Erase

37 min
Objectives
  • Retouch skin non-destructively on a separate empty layer
  • Remove temporary blemishes with the Spot Healing Brush and Healing Brush
  • Decide what to fix and what to leave: the ethics of a natural retouch
  • Dial back the work with layer opacity so it never looks overdone
Teaching sequence and demo script
  1. Frame: temporary vs permanent (2m). Say: "Great retouching is invisible. Your subject should look like themselves on their best day, not like a wax figure." Draw two columns on the board. Temporary (would it be there next week? no): a spot, a stray hair, shine, lint. Permanent: freckles, moles, laugh lines, scars, the shape of the nose. Say: "Heal the temporary, keep the permanent. When unsure, keep it. You can always reduce later."
  2. Demo: survey first (2m). Open l08-portrait-blemishes.jpg, File ▸ Save As → portrait-working.psd. Zoom to 100% (Ctrl/⌘ + 1) and pan slowly across the face without touching a tool. Think aloud: "Red spot on the cheek: temporary. Freckles across the nose: permanent. Mole by the lip: permanent. That dark hair across the forehead: temporary." Say: "This sixty-second survey is what separates natural from overdone."
  3. Demo: the retouch layer (3m). New empty layer, name it retouch. Spot Healing Brush (J): options bar Type: Content-Aware, tick Sample All Layers. Say: "It reads the face below but writes only on this layer. The original face is never touched."
  4. Demo: heal (3m). Size the brush just larger than a spot ([ and ] resize it) and single-click. Do three open-skin spots. Then a spot at the hairline: switch to the Healing Brush (Shift + J cycles the group). Say: "Different tool, different setting." Set Sample: Current & Below. Alt/⌥-click clean skin of the same brightness nearby, then click the spot. Say: "If it still smears the edge, finish it with the Clone Stamp, S, same Sample setting."
  5. Demo: dial back and check (2m). Lower the retouch layer's Opacity to about 85–90%. Toggle its 👁 off and on. Say: "With the layer on, is it still clearly the same person on a good day? Then we nailed it." Save.
Lab: Retouch a Portrait (20 min)

Learners work on their own consented portrait or l08-portrait-blemishes.jpg. They list their temporary and permanent items in the workbook before healing, then follow the lesson's four steps and save portrait-working.psd (the file carries through L09 and L10).

Circulate for: a named, empty retouch layer that is actually selected while healing; Sample All Layers ticked on Spot Healing; Sample: Current & Below on the Healing Brush; zoom at 100%; freckles and the mole still present.

Definition of done: the lesson checklist. All healing on the retouch layer; temporary blemishes gone; freckles, moles, and lines still there; opacity slightly under 100%; still the same person.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • "The brush does nothing." On an empty layer, Spot Healing without Sample All Layers (or the Healing Brush left on Sample: Current Layer) has nothing to read. Check the options bar for the tool that's active; the two tools use different settings.
  • Healing on the photo layer. The retouch layer was created but the Background stayed selected. Look at which layer is highlighted before the first click.
  • The clean sweep. Removing every freckle and line "while you're in there." That's the permanent bucket, and it's what makes a face look plastic. Point back at their written lists.
  • Color patches after healing. The source was a shadow or highlight. Sample from skin of the same brightness, or pick the source deliberately with the Healing Brush.
  • Brush far too big. A brush much larger than the spot drags in hairline, eyebrow, or lip texture. "Just larger than the blemish."
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time, else homework): the solo variation: retouch two copies, one tasteful and one deliberately overdone, and compare side by side. Then the "reduce, not erase" move: heal a blemish that sits on a freckle on its own new layer and lower that layer's opacity so a hint returns (lowering the main retouch layer would fade every heal at once).
  • Struggling learners: use the practice file (flaws are obvious and known) and only the Spot Healing Brush in open skin. Add the Healing Brush for the hairline spot once the basic click works.
Check for understanding: workbook self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 C) A red spot that wasn't there last week; Q2 A) The original face is never touched and the work can be reduced or deleted; Q3 B) Its Sample menu is on Current Layer; set it to Current & Below; Q4 C) Switch to the Healing Brush and Alt/⌥-click clean skin as the source; Q5 A) A whisper of the original keeps it believable; Q6 B) Survey at 100% and sort temporary vs permanent. Full explanations in the Answer Key. Journal prompt (homework): Where's your personal line between "cleanup" and "changing who someone is"?

L09

Frequency Separation

55 min
Objectives
  • Explain high frequency (texture) vs low frequency (color and tone)
  • Build a frequency-separation setup with Gaussian Blur + Apply Image
  • Even out blotchy skin tone on the color layer, texture untouched
  • Fix stray texture on the texture layer, color untouched
Teaching sequence and demo script
  1. Frame: two sheets of skin (3m). Say: "Healing fixes spots, but it can't even out blotchy tone. Blurring the skin turns it to plastic because blur smooths texture and color together. So we split them." Draw two stacked sheets: LOW = the paint (color, shading, blotches); HIGH = the texture stamped into it (pores, fine hairs). Say: "The words 'Apply Image' and 'Linear Light' sound scary. You set them once, as a recipe. Ninety percent of this technique is painting softly on the color layer."
  2. Demo: stamp and duplicate (2m). Open l09-portrait-blotchy.jpg (or continue portrait-working.psd). With the top layer selected, Stamp Visible: Ctrl + Alt + Shift + E / ⌘ + ⌥ + ⇧ + E. Duplicate it twice (Ctrl/⌘ + J, twice). Name the lower copy LOW, the upper copy HIGH. Hide HIGH.
  3. Demo: make LOW (3m). Select LOW. Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian Blur. Raise the radius slowly while watching the cheek. Say: "Stop when the blotches are gone and the pores just disappear. Often 6 to 12 pixels on a portrait. No more." Click OK.
  4. Demo: make HIGH (5m). Show and select HIGH. Image ▸ Apply Image. Read each setting aloud as you set it: Layer: LOW, Blending: Subtract, Scale: 2, Offset: 128. OK. Say: "Flat gray with faint texture: that's correct, don't panic." Set HIGH's blend mode to Linear Light. The face snaps back. Say: "Looks identical to the original. The difference is that texture and color now live on separate layers." Mention the 16-bit recipe only as a warning: Add, Scale 2, Offset 0, Invert.
  5. Demo: even the tone on LOW (4m). Select LOW. Lasso a loose loop around a blotchy cheek patch, Select ▸ Modify ▸ Feather 10–20 px, then a small Gaussian Blur. Deselect (Ctrl/⌘ + D). Then show the alternative: soft Brush at low opacity, Alt/⌥-click to sample good nearby color, paint gently. Toggle HIGH off to show the "paint" sheet, then on: the pores are still razor sharp.
  6. Demo: fix texture on HIGH (3m). Select HIGH. Healing Brush with Sample: Current Layer. Say: "Current Layer this time. Sampling other layers would drag color onto the texture sheet." Remove the flyaway hair. Save.
Lab: Separate & Smooth (30 min)

Learners build the setup on portrait-working.psd (or l09-portrait-blotchy.jpg if their own portrait has little uneven tone) with the recipe card in view, even the tone on LOW, fix one bit of texture on HIGH, and save.

Circulate for: the checkpoint after Linear Light: "Does it look identical to the original?" If yes, they're past the hard part. If not, stop and diagnose (see below) before any smoothing. Then check that learners are on the correct sheet for each fix.

Definition of done: the lesson checklist. LOW blurred, HIGH in Linear Light; identical at first; blotches evened on LOW; pores still crisp; stray texture fixed on HIGH without color shift.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Gray mess after Apply Image. Almost always one of: blend mode not set to Linear Light; Layer in Apply Image left on something other than LOW; or the wrong recipe for the bit depth. Check Image ▸ Mode: 8 Bits/Channel = Subtract / Scale 2 / Offset 128; 16 Bits/Channel = Add / Scale 2 / Offset 0 / Invert. Fastest fix: delete HIGH, duplicate the stamped layer again, redo the step.
  • LOW and HIGH swapped. Blurring the upper copy and applying the recipe to the lower one. HIGH must sit on top.
  • Blur too strong on LOW. The face loses its natural shading and looks flat. "Just enough that the blotches and pores vanish."
  • Hard edges in the smoothing. A Lasso selection without Feather leaves a visible outline. Feather 10–20 px first.
  • Working on the wrong sheet. Painting on HIGH smears texture; healing on LOW does nothing useful. Say it as a mantra: color on LOW, texture on HIGH.
  • Stamp Visible shortcut fails. All four keys at once; on a Mac it's ⌘⌥⇧E. If a learner still can't get it, have them select all layers, duplicate them, and merge the copies (Layer ▸ Merge Layers) as a fallback.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time, else homework): the solo variation: even only the forehead and cheeks; compare against a "lazy" Gaussian Blur on a duplicate of the whole face; then put the LOW smoothing inside a group with a black mask and paint it in only where needed. Stretch: convert a copy of the practice file to 16 Bits/Channel and run the 16-bit recipe.
  • Struggling learners: pair them with a neighbor who's past the Linear Light checkpoint, and have them work on l09-skin-swatch.jpg (a small, flat skin patch that makes the setup fast and the result obvious) before trying the face. Success = the checkpoint plus one evened patch. The HIGH fix is a bonus.
Check for understanding: workbook self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) Color and tone: the blotches and shading; Q2 A) Until the blotches are gone and the pores just disappear; Q3 C) Subtract, Scale 2, Offset 128, then Linear Light; Q4 A) HIGH's blend mode isn't Linear Light (or the wrong bit-depth recipe); Q5 B) Healing Brush or Clone Stamp on HIGH with Sample: Current Layer; Q6 C) Blur smooths texture and color together, so pores vanish. Full explanations in the Answer Key. Journal prompt (homework): How did it feel to pull off frequency separation? Note any step you'd want to re-read.

L10

Dodge & Burn: Sculpting Light

45 min
Objectives
  • Sculpt a face by dodging (lightening) and burning (darkening)
  • Do it non-destructively on a 50% gray Soft Light layer
  • Add subtle life to eyes and teeth with masked adjustments
  • Finish and export a complete, natural portrait retouch
Teaching sequence and demo script
  1. Frame: painting with light (2m). Say: "Darkroom printers held back light to lighten an area, dodge, and added light to darken it, burn. We're lighting artists adding highlights and shadows after the shot." Point at a face: brow, nose bridge, cheekbones, chin catch light; sides, eye sockets, under the jaw fall into shadow. Say: "This isn't new. It's non-destructive editing, black-and-white like a mask, aimed at light."
  2. Demo: the gray layer (2m). Open l10-portrait-flat.jpg (or continue the portrait). Alt/⌥-click the New Layer button. In the dialog set Mode: Soft Light, then tick Fill with Soft-Light-neutral color (50% gray). Name it D&B. Say: "It's invisible. That's correct: gray vanishes in Soft Light."
  3. Demo: dodge (3m). Brush (B), soft and large, Opacity ~10%. Press D, then X so white is the foreground. Several light passes down the nose bridge, forehead center, tops of the cheekbones, chin. Say: "Ten percent, many passes. You can always add more; you can't un-see a harsh streak."
  4. Demo: burn (3m). X for black. Sides of the face, temples, under the cheekbones, under the jaw, eye sockets. Point out the practice file's light comes from the left. Say: "Follow the existing light. Dodge the left-facing planes more, burn the right. Fighting the original light looks wrong instantly." Alt/⌥-click the D&B layer's 👁 to show the white/black "map" alone, then Alt/⌥-click again to return.
  5. Demo: eyes and teeth (3m). Add a Curves layer, brighten slightly, fill its mask black (Ctrl/⌘ + I inverts the white mask), paint white over the whites of the eyes and the teeth with a small soft brush. Say: "Clean, not glowing."
  6. Demo: finish (2m). Fit to screen (Ctrl/⌘ + 0), toggle D&B for before/after, File ▸ Save the .psd, then File ▸ Export ▸ Export As ▸ JPG about 80% → portrait-final.jpg.
Lab: Sculpt & Finish (25 min)

On their portrait (or l10-portrait-flat.jpg), learners first write down where the light comes from, then build the D&B layer, dodge and burn, add the masked Curves for eyes and teeth, save the PSD, and export portrait-final.jpg. If time: the optional second Curves layer masked to the irises for a little sparkle.

Circulate for: Soft Light mode and the 50% gray fill on the D&B layer; brush opacity around 10% (check the options bar, not just the stroke); strokes following the photo's light direction; the D&B layer selected while painting (not the portrait).

Definition of done: the lesson checklist. D&B on a 50% gray Soft Light layer; highlights and shadows follow the existing light; more three-dimensional, not painted-on; eyes and teeth subtly brightened with masked Curves; PSD saved and JPG exported.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • The gray layer shows as solid gray. The mode was left on Normal. Change the layer's blend mode to Soft Light. (If the layer is transparent instead, they skipped the "Fill with Soft-Light-neutral color" checkbox, which only becomes available after choosing Soft Light in the dialog. Fill it with 50% gray via Edit ▸ Fill ▸ Contents: 50% Gray, or remake the layer.)
  • Harsh streaks. Opacity at 100% or a hard brush. Undo, set ~10%, and use a big soft brush.
  • Painting on the portrait. White and black paint appears directly on the face. Undo and select the D&B layer.
  • Inventing light. Dodging the shadow side "because it looks dark." Enhance what's there.
  • Glowing teeth, alien eyes. The eyes-and-teeth Curves pushed too far, or the brush spilled onto lips and skin. Pull the curve back; refine the mask with black.
  • Reaching for the Dodge and Burn tools. They paint directly on pixels (destructive). Fine on a duplicate for a quick fix, but the gray layer is the pro default because it stays re-editable.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time, else homework): the solo variation: gray layer only (no eyes/teeth step) to see how far light alone carries it; duplicate the D&B layer to see "too much," then dial opacity back to taste; burn a subtle vignette around the frame on the same layer.
  • Struggling learners: dodge only the nose bridge and cheekbones, burn only under the cheekbones and jaw, then export. Three zones done well is a finished portrait. Skip the iris layer.
Check for understanding: workbook self-check + journal

Answers: Q1 B) Lightens (dodges) that area; Q2 C) 50% gray is invisible in Soft Light, so only your strokes show, and it stays re-editable; Q3 A) Large, soft brush at about 10% opacity, many passes; Q4 B) Dodge the left-facing planes, burn the right; Q5 C) A slightly brightened Curves layer, mask filled black, white painted over eye whites and teeth; Q6 A) The tools change pixels directly; the gray layer is re-editable.   • Kill the tell-tale fringe with Decontaminate Colors
  • Output the result as a layer with a mask, ready to drop into a scene
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: the background tells on you (3m). Open l11-figure-on-green.png, do a quick Select Subject and plain layer mask, then add a bright orange Solid Color fill layer underneath (Layer ▸ New Fill Layer ▸ Solid Color). Zoom to 200% on the hair. Say: "See that green halo? When this person was photographed, the edge pixels picked up color from what was behind them. Move them to a sunset and that green screams 'pasted.' Today we clean it out." Delete this quick attempt.
  2. Select, then Select and Mask (4m). Say: "You already made selections and masks in Lesson 1.2. Same tools, higher stakes." Click: Select ▸ Subject. Then Select ▸ Select and Mask… (Ctrl+Alt+R / ⌘+⌥+R), or the Select and Mask… button in any selection tool's options bar. Set View to On Layers or Overlay. Say: "Pick a view that lets you judge the edge honestly. Overlay shows me exactly what is and isn't selected."
  3. Rescue the hair (6m). Click: Refine Hair in the options bar. Under Edge Detection tick Smart Radius and raise Radius a few pixels. Then take the Refine Edge Brush and paint along the wispy strands still missing. Set Shift Edge slightly negative. Say: "Smooth and Feather are optional. Both soften detail, so on hair I keep them at or near zero." Then deliberately drag the Refine Edge Brush across the shoulder and show the mush. Say: "Hair only. Solid edges stay solid."
  4. Decontaminate (4m). Click: open Output Settings, tick Decontaminate Colors, raise Amount while zoomed on the hair until the green halo disappears. Set Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask, click OK. Say: "Notice Photoshop forces a new layer. Decontamination rewrites edge pixels, so it can't stay fully non-destructive. That's why we keep the original."
  5. Believability test + bank it (1m). Show the orange fill behind the new cutout: the edge stays clean. Group the cutout layer with its mask, name the group SUBJECT, leave the original photo hidden beneath, save as composite-working.psd.
Lab: A Composite-Ready Cutout (24 min)

Learners cut out their own subject (portrait, product, or pet on a plain-ish background) or labs/module-4/practice/l11-figure-on-green.png, following Steps 1–4 of the guided build, and save composite-working.psd. Fast finishers move on to l11-pet-on-blue-wall.png (fur edge). Full spec in labs/module-4/README.md.

Definition of done (the lesson's checklist): subject on its own layer with a mask (background hidden, not deleted); hair soft and natural; no colored fringe against a saturated test background; original kept as a hidden backup; group named SUBJECT and saved.

Circulate for: Output To still on "Selection" or "Layer Mask" (Decontaminate grays those out, so check the box is actually ticked); people painting a normal brush on hair; people judging the edge at 25% zoom.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Over-brushing: running the Refine Edge Brush along a jaw or shoulder turns crisp detail to mush. Hair and fur only.
  • Expecting the brush to invent hair: it rebuilds strands at the edge; it cannot recover hair the original selection missed. If a big clump is gone, go back and fix the selection first.
  • Smooth/Feather cranked up to "fix" a jagged edge: the whole outline goes soft and looks pasted. Near 0 on hair.
  • Deleting the background instead of masking it, or deleting the original layer. Keep both.
  • Skipping decontamination because the cutout "looks fine" on the original background. It always looks fine there. Test against a saturated color.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): the solo variation: cut out the fur subject (l11-pet-on-blue-wall.png), test against three different colored fills, then zoom to 200% and inspect the hair edge pixel by pixel. Challenge: curly hair, fur, or a glass object from their own photos.
  • Struggling learners: switch them to l11-figure-on-green.png (built to cut cleanly), set View to Overlay for them, and reduce the task to three moves: Select Subject, tick Decontaminate, Output To New Layer with Layer Mask. Hair refinement can come later.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 B) edge pixels carry color from the old background; Q2 B) it replaces the old background's color in the edge pixels; Q3 C) New Layer with Layer Mask; Q4 B) wispy hair and fur only; Q5 A) drop a saturated colored layer behind the cutout; Q6 C) Ctrl+Alt+R / ⌘+⌥+R. Full explanations in the Answer Key. Journal prompt (write now if time, otherwise homework): Before today, would you have noticed a fringe on a cutout? Note one composite in the wild (an ad, a meme, a movie poster) where the cutout betrays itself.

L12

Make It Belong: Match Light & Color

45 min
Objectives
  • Diagnose why a composite looks "pasted" by checking brightness, color, and contrast
  • Match the subject to the scene with clipped adjustment layers
  • Pull the scene's color into the subject with Curves and Color Balance
  • Use a clipping mask so each adjustment affects only the subject
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: speak the scene's language (3m). Say: "Every object in a real scene is lit by the same sources, so they share an accent: the same warmth, brightness range, softness. A pasted subject speaks with a foreign accent." Write on the board: 1 Brightness · 2 Color · 3 Contrast. Say: "In that order. Brightness alone is usually eighty percent of the 'pasted' feeling."
  2. Bring in the scene (2m). In composite-working.psd: File ▸ Place Embedded → l12-scene-sunset.jpg, press Enter to accept, drag its layer below the SUBJECT group.
  3. Position and size (2m). Right-click the SUBJECT group ▸ Convert to Smart Object. Ctrl+T / ⌘+T, scale to a believable size. Say: "Where would this person actually stand? Feet on the ground, not floating."
  4. Brightness first (4m). Add a Curves adjustment layer above the subject, clip it with Ctrl+Alt+G / ⌘+⌥+G (or Alt/⌥-click the line between the layers). Point at the indent and bent arrow (↳) and the underlined name of the layer it clips to. Drag the curve down until the subject sits in the scene's brightness range. Say: "Squint. Squinting hides detail and shows brightness mismatches."
  5. Color temperature (4m). Add a clipped Color Balance; push toward red and yellow in the midtones and highlights for the sunset. Then switch Tone to Shadows and nudge slightly cooler. Say: "Warm light usually means cool shadows. Warm everything evenly and the subject looks dipped in orange."
  6. Contrast and saturation (2m). If the scene is soft and hazy, flatten the subject's contrast a touch with the clipped Curves. Add a clipped Hue/Saturation and pull Saturation down a few points.
  7. Squint-and-flick (1m). Toggle all clipped layers off and on. Say: "The 'off' version should now look obviously wrong. If it does, you matched well."
Lab: Match the Subject (24 min)

Learners place a background (their own, or labs/module-4/practice/l12-scene-sunset.jpg) under their L11 cutout and follow Steps 1–4: Smart Object + scale, clipped Curves (brightness), clipped Color Balance (temperature), clipped Curves/Hue/Saturation (contrast and saturation). Fast finishers repeat the match on l12-scene-overcast.jpg.

Definition of done: subject sized and positioned believably (feet on the ground); brightness matches nearby scene elements; lit side carries the scene's color and shadows are not over-warmed; contrast and saturation match; every adjustment is clipped, leaving the background untouched.

Circulate for: adjustment layers without the ↳ hook (the background changed color too); adjustments placed below the subject; subjects that are simply too large for the scene.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Forgetting to clip. The most common error of the day. Tell them: "If your sky changed color, you forgot to clip." Have them check for the ↳ on every adjustment.
  • Starting with color. People love the Color Balance sliders and ignore brightness. Enforce the order.
  • Scaling the raw layer many times before converting to a Smart Object, softening it. Convert first, then scale.
  • "Dipped in orange." Warming the whole subject evenly. Cool the shadows a touch.
  • Clipping confusion: a clipping mask is not a layer mask. Clipping restricts an adjustment to the shape of the layer directly below; a layer mask hides parts of a layer (black hides, white reveals).
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): match the same cutout into both the sunset and the overcast scene and line them up; try the hard case (l12-subject-indoor-warm.png, a tungsten-lit subject, into daylight); add the light wrap: duplicate the background (Ctrl/⌘+J), drag it above the subject and clip it, Gaussian Blur roughly 20–40 px, Screen or Soft Light at low opacity, black mask, paint white only along the edges facing the light. Local matches: a second clipped set masked to just the clothing or just the skin.
  • Struggling learners: brightness only. One clipped Curves, squint, done. That alone does most of the work; add color when brightness is right. Give them a shortcut card: Ctrl+Alt+G / ⌘+⌥+G = clip.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 A) brightness, then color, then contrast; Q2 C) it affects only the layer directly below; Q3 B) Ctrl/⌘+Alt/⌥+G; Q4 B) add a touch of cooler color to the shadow side; Q5 A) so resizing never degrades it; Q6 C) sample a neutral surface / read highlights and shadows. Journal prompt: Which of the three (brightness, color, contrast) was hardest for your eye to judge?

L13

Ground It & Grade It

55 min
Objectives
  • Anchor a subject with a contact shadow and a soft cast shadow
  • Add depth with atmospheric perspective (haze)
  • Tie everything together with a single unifying grade
  • Save a layered master and export a flattened, finished, believable composite
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: object, space, whole (3m). Say: "Three cues convince the eye. Shadow ties the object to the ground. Depth, haze, restores the space. One grade over everything makes it feel shot on one camera." Point out the spiral: masks (Module 1), Curves (Module 2), shadow painting that is really dodge and burn (Module 3). Draw the finishing stack on the board: grade on top · haze · SUBJECT · contact shadow · cast shadow · background.
  2. Contact shadow (5m). New layer below the subject, name it Contact Shadow. Soft black brush, low opacity, paint a small dark pool right where the feet touch the ground. Say: "Darkest and tightest at the contact point. This layer never gets blurred."
  3. Cast shadow (5m). Second new layer just below Contact Shadow, name it Cast Shadow. Point at the scene's existing shadows first. Say: "The sun here is on the right, so the shadow falls to the left. One light, one shadow direction." Paint a longer, softer shadow away from the light, lowering brush opacity as you move outward, then Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian Blur on this layer only.
  4. Atmosphere (2m). Only if the subject sits in the mid or far distance: a low-opacity soft layer of the scene's haze color, masked to the distance. Say: "Haze is a whisper. If you can see it, it's too strong."
  5. Unify (3m). At the very top of the stack, a Color Lookup or gentle Curves for one shared mood. Vignette: new layer, big soft black brush on the corners, lower the layer's opacity until the edges quietly darken.
  6. Save and export (1m). File ▸ Save the layered master (do not flatten). File ▸ Export ▸ Export As, choose JPG. Say: "Export flattens automatically. Your .psd stays fully layered."
Lab: Ground, Depth, Grade (32 min), Module 4 deliverable

Learners finish their L12 composite with Steps 1–4 and export a JPG. Fast finishers use labs/module-4/practice/l13-scene-hard-sun.jpg (hard sun) against l12-scene-overcast.jpg (soft light) to compare shadows.

Definition of done: a dark, tight contact shadow where the subject meets the ground; a softer, blurred cast shadow on its own layer falling the same way as the scene's other shadows; distance reads through subtle atmosphere (where relevant); one unifying grade over the whole stack; layered master saved and JPG exported.

Circulate for: shadows painted inside the SUBJECT Smart Object or above it; the Gaussian Blur run on the contact-shadow layer; the unifying grade clipped to the subject (it must sit unclipped at the top); learners who flattened and saved over their master.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Wrong shadow direction. Always check the background's own shadows before painting. Sun high-right means the shadow falls low-left.
  • Uniform shadow. One flat gray blob looks fake. Darkest and sharpest at contact, softer and lighter as it travels.
  • Heavy haze and heavy grade. Both are cohesion tools, not drama tools. If the grade is doing the heavy lifting, the L12 match needs work.
  • Flattening the master. Save the .psd first; Export As handles the flattened JPG.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): finish one composite under hard light and one under soft light and compare shadows (hard light: sharp, dark; soft light: faint, fuzzy); point the shadow the wrong way on purpose, then fix it; grade the same composite warm and golden, then cool and moody. Master's touch: reflected light, a little bounced scene color (green grass under a chin) on the shadow side.
  • Struggling learners: contact shadow + unifying grade only, then save and export. The contact shadow is the single strongest cue; a composite with just that and one grade is a legitimate deliverable. Add the cast shadow if time allows.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 B) a contact shadow; Q2 A) so it can be blurred without softening the contact point; Q3 C) low-left; Q4 B) hazier, lighter, lower in contrast; Q5 A) at the very top of the stack, over everything; Q6 B) save the layered .psd, then Export As JPG. Journal prompt: Of shadow, atmosphere, and grade, which made the biggest difference to your composite?

Gallery & Critique Protocol (13 min)

  1. Each learner puts their exported JPG full-screen for about 45 seconds (in a large room, a handful of volunteers).
  2. The room squints together and answers three questions aloud: Where is the light coming from? Do the shadows agree? Does the subject pop out or melt in?
  3. The maker names one thing they are proud of and one thing they would fix next.
  4. Keep feedback specific and kind: "the contact shadow sells it; the cast shadow could be softer at the tip" rather than "looks fake."

Closing the Module

Module 4 wrap-up (5 min)

Recap the arc: a fringe-free cutout → a subject matched to the scene's light → a grounded, graded, single photograph. Everyone should leave with a layered composite-working.psd and an exported JPG. Encourage: "You just made an image that never existed look like one frame. That took every module so far." Preview Module 5: "Next session we design: type, shapes, and layout, as you build a poster. Bring your composite; it can be the poster's hero image."

L14

Type & Hierarchy

45 min
Objectives
  • Set and edit text with the Type tool (point type vs paragraph type)
  • Build a clear visual hierarchy with size, weight, and spacing
  • Control tracking, kerning, and leading in the Character panel
  • Curve text along a shape with type on a path
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: type has a job (3m). Put l14-flat-type-before.png on screen. Ask: "What do you read first?" (Nobody agrees: that's the problem.) Say: "Every line has a job: grab attention, give the key detail, or be the fine print. Three tools do almost all the work: size, weight, space. 'I have no eye for design' usually means nobody told you the rules."
  2. New poster (2m). File ▸ New, 18 × 24 inches at 150 ppi, dark background. Save as poster-working.psd. Say: "We'll build this one file all session."
  3. Get the words down (4m). Type tool (T), click once, type SUMMER. Commit with Ctrl+Enter / ⌘+Enter, Esc, or the ✓ in the options bar. Click an empty spot for each next line (MUSIC FESTIVAL, July 18 · Riverside Park, Live bands · Food trucks · All ages, TICKETS AT THE DOOR). Say: "Clicking on existing text edits it. Click empty canvas for a new layer. Single click = point type, for short display lines. Click-and-drag a box = paragraph type, for body text that wraps."
  4. Assign jobs and size boldly (3m). Say the reading order aloud: name 1st, date and place 2nd, details 3rd, fine print last. Scale the title up until it feels uncomfortably big, then a bit more. Say: "Weak hierarchy is the most common beginner mistake. If your 1st and 2nd lines are close in size, it's too weak."
  5. Character panel (2m). Window ▸ Character. Tighten leading on the two-line title so it reads as one unit; add positive tracking (for example +120) to the small all-caps label. Say: "Leading = between lines. Tracking = evenly across a word or line. Kerning = between one specific pair of letters."
  6. Type on a path (1m). Ellipse tool, mode set to Path in the options bar, drag a circle. Hover the Type tool over the path until the cursor shows a wavy line, click, type EST · 2026 · LIVE. Drag with the Path Selection tool to slide or flip it. Say: "Upside-down on the bottom of the circle? Normal. Drag across the path to flip it."
Lab: Fix the Hierarchy (24 min)

Learners build their poster text in poster-working.psd from labs/module-5/practice/l14-poster-copy.txt (or their own event), following Steps 1–4: every line its own layer, a reading order, bold sizing, deliberate leading and tracking, one accent color, and one piece of type on a path.

Definition of done (the lesson's checklist): every line is its own type layer with a clear job; the 1st line is boldly bigger; leading and tracking are set deliberately; one or two fonts and one accent color; at least one element uses type on a path.

Circulate for: all text in one layer; a title set as paragraph type in a too-small box (overflow hidden); three or more fonts; an accent color on every line.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Timid title. The fix on most beginner posters: "make the main thing much larger."
  • Font circus. Two fonts maximum: one characterful display font for the title, one clean font for the rest. Contrast through size and weight.
  • Paragraph type for a title. If the box is too small the overflow is hidden, and resizing the box doesn't scale the letters. Use point type for display lines.
  • Typing into the wrong layer. Clicking on existing text edits it. Commit first, then click empty canvas.
  • Crowding. White space is not wasted space; it's what makes the important things feel important.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): set the same text three ways changing only size and spacing (three moods, one font, no color); recreate the hierarchy of a poster or album cover they admire; curve a tagline into a full circle around a logo; set the headline in a serif, a sans, and a script and compare the personality.
  • Struggling learners: reduce to three lines (title, date/place, fine print) and one font. Get the size jump right first; spacing and type on a path can wait. If typing new layers keeps going wrong, have them press Esc after each line as a ritual.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 A) point type; Q2 B) the space between lines; Q3 C) add positive tracking; Q4 A) make the main line much bigger; Q5 B) one or two; Q6 C) drag across the path with the Path Selection tool to flip it. Full explanations in the Answer Key. Journal prompt: Did "I have no eye for design" ever apply to you? Write down the one rule that surprised you most.

L15

Shapes & the Pen

45 min
Objectives
  • Explain vector vs raster, and why vectors never pixelate
  • Draw and combine shape layers (rectangles, ellipses, custom shapes)
  • Use the Pen tool to make clean straight and curved paths
  • Turn paths into shapes, selections, or masks
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: math, not pixels (3m). Say: "A photo is a grid of colored squares. Enlarge it and there are no new squares, so edges go soft or blocky. A vector is stored as instructions: 'a circle of this radius here.' Photoshop redraws it fresh at any size. Type is vector too. Photos are raster; logos, icons, frames, badges, and type should be vector."
  2. A frame (2m). Rectangle tool (U), mode Shape. Draw a border rectangle. Options bar: Fill = none (the white box with a red slash, No Color), Stroke = a few pixels in the accent color.
  3. A badge with Path Operations (3m). Draw an Ellipse shape. With that shape layer still active, open the Path Operations menu in the options bar and choose Subtract Front Shape, then draw a smaller circle. Say: "Set the operation before drawing the next shape. Hold Shift to add or Alt/⌥ to subtract as you start dragging. On the default New Layer setting, the second shape lands on its own layer instead." Show the shape layer's thumbnail badge; double-click the thumbnail to recolor.
  4. The Pen's two-move rule (5m). Pen (P), mode Shape. On l15-pen-practice.png: trace the lightning bolt with clicks only. Say: "Straight line: click, click." Then the leaf: click-and-drag to pull out handles. Say: "Curve: click and drag, then click and drag again. That's 90% of the Pen." Nudge an anchor and a handle with the Direct Selection tool (white arrow). Say: "One anchor wherever the curve changes direction, and no more."
  5. Paths do triple duty (2m). Switch the Pen to Path mode, draw a closed path. Show it in the Paths panel as a Work Path, not a layer. With the Pen still active, right-click the path: Make Selection, Fill Path, Stroke Path, Create Vector Mask. Choose Make Selection, then Add Layer Mask. Say: "Straight back to Module 1. And for any shape layer, Ctrl/⌘-click its thumbnail to load it as a selection."
  6. The scale test (1m). Duplicate a small shape layer, right-click the copy ▸ Rasterize Layer. Scale both about 400% with Ctrl/⌘+T; view at 100%. Say: "Just zooming won't show it: Photoshop draws both as screen pixels at high zoom. The difference appears when you resize."
Lab: Vector Elements (23 min)

In poster-working.psd: a stroked frame with no fill, a badge from combined shapes (with the L14 type on a path around it), one custom Pen shape (a mountain, a soundwave, a leaf), one path reused as a selection or mask, and the scale test. Warm up on labs/module-5/practice/l15-pen-practice.png first (5 min max).

Definition of done: a vector frame with a stroke and no fill; a badge built from combined shapes; one custom Pen shape with few, clean anchor points; shape edges crisp after scaling up 400% (the rasterized copy is not); at least one path reused as a selection or mask.

Circulate for: a Pen left in Path mode when they wanted a shape (nothing appears in Layers); dozens of anchor points; frames with a solid fill covering the poster; badges on two separate layers because Path Operations was set after drawing.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Dragging the Pen like a brush. It places points; it doesn't paint. Demonstrate the wrong way once so they recognize it.
  • Too many anchor points = lumpy curves. Also check that handles run roughly tangent to the curve.
  • Wrong mode. Shape mode makes a layer; Path mode makes a Work Path; Pixels mode is raster. Check the options bar first.
  • Rasterizing to "fix" something. Rasterizing throws away crisp-at-any-size. Only for pixel edits, and keep a vector copy.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): the "Pen game" (heart, cloud, lightning bolt with as few points as possible); rebuild l15-logo-to-rebuild.png with Shape tools and Path Operations; trace a hard-edged subject (a bottle, a car) with the Pen instead of Select & Mask.
  • Struggling learners: Pen straight lines only today (the mountain range or lightning bolt: clicks only). Build the frame and badge with Shape tools, which need no Pen at all. Curves become homework with the practice sheet.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 B) it is stored as math and redrawn at any size; Q2 A) click for corners, click-and-drag for curves; Q3 C) too many anchor points; Q4 B) a Work Path in the Paths panel; Q5 A) scale both up about 400% and compare at 100%; Q6 C) with the shape layer active, before drawing the second shape. Journal prompt: After the two-move rule, does the Pen feel less mysterious? Note the shape you found hardest to trace and why.

L16

Layer Styles & the Poster

58 min
Objectives
  • Apply layer styles (Drop Shadow, Stroke, Gradient Overlay) as live effects
  • Copy, paste, and reuse styles across layers
  • Use Smart Objects for logos and repeated graphics
  • Assemble a poster and export correctly for print vs screen
Teaching sequence + live-demo script
  1. Frame: live effects (3m). Say: "A layer style lives on a layer, not painted into it. Change the text and the shadow follows. It's the same non-destructive idea as adjustment layers and Smart Filters from Module 2, applied to effects." Then the warning: "Overdone layer styles are the fastest way to make work look dated. One or two effects usually beats five."
  2. Lift the title (4m). Double-click the title layer to the right of its name to open Layer Style. Tick Gradient Overlay, pick a two-tone version of the accent. Tick Drop Shadow (it's at the bottom of the list), click its name to see its controls: low opacity, small distance, moderate size. Edit the title text afterward to show the effects follow live.
  3. Style and reuse (3m). Give the badge a Stroke and a soft Drop Shadow. Right-click its fx ▸ Copy Layer Style; right-click another layer ▸ Paste Layer Style. Say: "Consistency is what makes a design feel intentional."
  4. Smart Objects and a hero image (3m). File ▸ Place Embedded l16-sponsor-logo.png (it comes in as a Smart Object), scale it freely. Place a hero photo (l16-hero-stage.jpg, or a learner's Module 4 composite) behind the type.
  5. Assemble (1m). Move tool with smart guides; even margins; squint. Say: "The title dominates, the accent points to the key action, nothing is crowded against the edges."
  6. Export: screen vs print (4m). Screen: File ▸ Export ▸ Export As ▸ JPG (PNG if you need transparency), RGB, pixel dimensions that fit the platform. Print: Say: "First, ask the printer for their spec sheet: CMYK profile or PDF/X preset, and bleed, often 0.125 in / 3 mm per side. Photoshop has no bleed setting, so you build it in." Show Image ▸ Canvas Size (add the bleed on every side, extend the background into it, keep type well inside the trim), Edit ▸ Convert to Profile on a copy, File ▸ Save a Copy as Photoshop PDF with the printer's preset. Mention soft-proofing: View ▸ Proof Setup and View ▸ Proof Colors (Ctrl/⌘+Y). Say: "Keep your layered .psd as the master. Always."
Lab: Finish the Poster (34 min), Module 5 deliverable

Learners complete Steps 1–4 on poster-working.psd and export a screen version (JPG). Assets: labs/module-5/practice/l16-hero-stage.jpg and l16-sponsor-logo.png, or their own Module 4 composite and graphics. The print-ready PDF is homework unless time allows.

Definition of done (the lesson's checklist): the title has a gradient and a subtle drop shadow; a consistent style is reused (copy/paste layer style); reusable graphics are Smart Objects; effects are restrained (two or three per layer at most); everything aligned with even margins; a screen JPG exported and viewed at full size.

Circulate for: hard, black, offset drop shadows; bevel-and-glow stacks; a logo that went soft because it was placed as pixels and scaled; a flattened master.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Heavy shadow. Lower opacity, reduce distance, raise size a little. If you can clearly see the shadow as a separate object, dial it back.
  • Styles that don't scale. Transforming a layer doesn't resize its effects, so a stroke can look too thin or too heavy afterward. Right-click the fx ▸ Scale Effects (or Layer ▸ Layer Style ▸ Scale Effects). Image ▸ Image Size scales them only when Scale Styles is ticked.
  • "Print looks different." Screens are RGB and lit; print is CMYK ink on paper and can't hit every screen color. Design in RGB, soft-proof, confirm with the printer. Neons shift most.
  • Exporting at 300 ppi for Instagram. The ppi number does not matter on screen; pixel dimensions do.
Differentiation
  • Fast learners (if time / homework): restyle the poster for a different mood and watch reused styles carry the change; build a matching square social version reusing the same styles and Smart Objects; over-style one copy deliberately next to the tasteful one; save a favorite title treatment as a reusable Style in the Styles panel; produce the print-ready PDF with bleed.
  • Struggling learners: title gradient + drop shadow, one copied style, hero image behind, export JPG. That meets the core of the deliverable; Smart Object logo and print output follow as homework.
Check for understanding (workbook self-check) + journal

Answers: Q1 C) live, re-editable effects attached to the layer; Q2 A) right-click fx ▸ Copy Layer Style, then Paste Layer Style; Q3 B) Gradient Overlay + a subtle Drop Shadow; Q4 A) RGB, JPG or PNG, pixel dimensions for the platform; Q5 C) Image ▸ Canvas Size, extending the background into the bleed; Q6 B) Scale Effects. Journal prompt: How does designing (type, shapes, layout) feel different from editing photos? Which did you enjoy more, and what does that tell you about where to go next?

Lesson Plans

L17

Generative Fill

40 min
Objectives
  • Remove an object by selecting it and generating with an empty prompt
  • Add new content by selecting an area and describing what belongs there
  • Work with generative layers and cycle through variations
  • Write prompts that work, and know Generative Fill's limits
Teaching sequence & demo script
  1. Hook (2m). Show the lesson's before/after (sign removed, sailboat added). Say: "Two edits, two prompts, and not one original pixel touched. Today you learn to drive this, and to know when not to."
  2. Mental model (3m). Write on the board: Select the where → describe the what → Generate → pick a variation → refine. Say: "Empty prompt removes. A described prompt adds. The result is a new generative layer with its own mask, so it is one more non-destructive layer, the principle from Module 0 all over again."
  3. Demo: the task bar (2m). Open l17-lake-sign.jpg, save as generative-working.psd. Click: Lasso around the sign with a little margin. Point at the floating Contextual Task Bar. Say: "No task bar? Window ▸ Contextual Task Bar, or use Edit ▸ Generative Fill instead."
  4. Demo: remove (3m). Click: Generative Fill → leave the prompt empty → Generate. Show the new generative layer in the Layers panel and cycle the three variations. Say: "This is the smarter cousin of Content-Aware Fill from Module 1."
  5. Demo: add (4m). Click: a tall-ish selection over open water → Generative Fill → type a small sailboat → Generate. Say: "Want a tall thing? Make a tall selection. Selecting the right space is half the prompt." Then click Generate again for three more, to show iteration is cheap.
  6. Demo: refine (2m). Paint on the generative layer's mask to soften a rough edge; clip a Curves layer to it to match brightness. Say: "Generative is a strong start, not always a finish. Your craft finishes the job."
  7. Prompts, limits, ethics (2m). Concrete and short ("a wooden park bench," not "something nice to sit on"); describe the thing, not the style; tweak one or two words rather than rewriting. Limits: hands, text, small faces; very large generations; needs internet and credits; results vary. Honesty: Content Credentials flag AI edits; journalism, evidence, and documentary work have a line.
Lab: Remove & Add (22 min)

Learners use their own photo (a clear distraction plus some open space) or l17-lake-sign.jpg, saved as generative-working.psd. Steps 1–3 of the lesson: remove, add, blend and refine. Fast finishers take l17-three-distractions.jpg and remove all three, one selection at a time. See labs/module-6/README.md.

Circulate for: each result on its own generative layer (original Background untouched); loose selections with margin, not tight outlines; learners actually cycling variations before accepting; at least one refine move (mask, clipped adjustment, or healing on a layer).

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • "Generative Fill is grayed out." Not signed in, no internet, or an old Photoshop version. Check sign-in first (if the Help menu offers "Sign In…", they are signed out); do not debug for more than two minutes, switch to the fallback plan.
  • Selection hugging the object exactly. Removal leaves halos and edge ghosts. Loose selection, a little margin.
  • Long, stylistic prompts. "A beautiful cinematic golden-hour yacht in watercolor style" fights the scene's light. Name the object; let the scene set the light.
  • Accepting the first variation. Make "look at all three" a rule.
  • Blurry, low-detail result. Selection was too large. Generate in smaller pieces, or regenerate.
  • Adding text or hands on purpose and expecting it to work. Save this for the solo "hard prompt" experiment, framed as learning its weak spots.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) Select an area, then describe what should be there; Q2 C) Leave the prompt empty and click Generate; Q3 A) On a new generative layer with its own mask; Q4 B) "a wooden park bench"; Q5 C) Make a taller selection. Cold-call: "Where would you draw your line between cleaning up a photo and changing its truth?" Then 3–5 minutes for the Learning Journal prompt.

Differentiation
  • Fast: the solo challenge: three removals on one busy photo, regenerate the same selection with a different prompt, one deliberately hard prompt (hands or readable text). Going further: treat a generated element as a real subject, then mask, grade, and add a shadow (Module 4).
  • Struggling: do only Step 1 (remove) until it is solid, on the practice file with the sign. Give them the recipe card and let them repeat it on two more distractions before attempting "add."
L18

Generative Expand & Reframe

34 min
Objectives
  • Extend the canvas with the Crop tool and fill the new space generatively
  • Uncrop a too-tight photo to give a subject room to breathe
  • Reframe a photo to a new aspect ratio for print, web, or social
  • Guide the fill with an optional prompt and keep the seam invisible
Teaching sequence & demo script
  1. Frame (2m). Say: "Sometimes the problem is not inside the photo, it is the edges. Expand is Generative Fill aimed at the empty canvas around your image. Same layer, same variations, same optional prompt. If you understood L17, you already understand this."
  2. Demo: uncrop (4m). Open l18-tight-portrait.jpg, save as expand-working.psd. Click: Crop tool (C) → options bar Fill: Generative Expand → drag the side handle outward past the photo. Leave the task bar prompt empty → Generate. Say: "The Crop tool can crop outward. Pressing Enter or the checkmark may also generate straight away with no prompt, so use the task bar when you want to type one."
  3. Demo: reframe to a ratio (3m). Undo, then pick Ratio 16:9. Point out the box first fits inside the photo (which would crop it). Click: drag the handles outward until the box extends past the edges; the ratio stays locked. Generate. Say: "A vertical phone photo just became a banner, and nothing was stretched."
  4. Expand in bites (2m). Show one huge expansion going vague and repetitive, then redo it in two or three smaller steps. The comparison sells the habit.
  5. Seam check (3m). Zoom to 100% along the join. Run the horizon test: does the horizon or any strong line jump? If so, cycle variations; if none line up, select a thin strip across the seam and regenerate just that with Generative Fill (L17). Finish with a subtle clipped Curves or a whisper of the Module 4 unifying grade over the whole frame.
Lab: Uncrop & Reframe (20 min)

On their own cramped photo or l18-tight-portrait.jpg: Step 1 uncrop, Step 2 reframe to one ratio (16:9, 1:1, or 4:5), Step 3 optional prompt ("open sky," "more forest"), Step 4 hide the seam. Seam-test file: l18-horizon-strip.jpg. See labs/module-6/README.md.

Circulate for: Fill set to Generative Expand (not Background); handles dragged past the photo, not just a ratio chosen; horizon continuous across the seam at 100%; no stretching (circles stay round).

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Choosing a ratio and pressing Enter. The box sits inside the photo, so Photoshop crops instead of expanding. Ratio sets only the shape; you still drag outward.
  • Fill left on Background. The new space fills with the background color (flat white or black). Check the options bar before dragging.
  • One giant leap. Smeary, repetitive margins. Expand partway, generate, repeat.
  • Using Free Transform to "widen" the photo. That stretches the subject. Expand invents new space instead.
  • Judging at fit-to-screen. Seams hide at small zoom. The check happens at 100%.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 A) Generative Fill aimed at empty canvas around the photo; Q2 C) Drag the crop handles outward past the photo; Q3 B) Expand in smaller steps, generating each time; Q4 B) A horizon or strong line that jumps at the seam; Q5 A) Continue the existing scene. Journal prompt: when does room to breathe improve a photo, and when does it dilute a shot that was strong because it was tight?

Differentiation
  • Fast: one vertical photo to three versions (1:1, 16:9, 9:16) by expanding; or expand a portrait for headroom and drop a title into it (Module 5 type). Going further: expand, then generatively add a new element into the new space.
  • Struggling: uncrop one side only with an empty prompt, then do the horizon test. Skip ratios until that feels easy.

Objectives

  • Run an Action across a folder with Batch
  • Resize and convert a whole shoot with the Image Processor

Teaching sequence & demo script
  1. Frame (2m). Say: "Generative tools make hard edits easy. Automation makes repetitive edits vanish. The rule of the lesson: if you do it more than twice, record it. Automate the boring, keep the creative."

    1. Edit ▸ Convert to Profile → destination sRGB IEC61966-2.1
    2. File ▸ Automate ▸ Fit Image → 1200 × 1200 px. Say: "Fit Image fits the long edge for landscape and portrait alike. Image Size would record a fixed width, which goes wrong on portrait files."
    3. File ▸ Place Embedded → l19-watermark.png, position it, commit
    4. Layer ▸ Flatten Image. Say: "Current Photoshop only offers JPEG in Save As for a file without extra layers."
    5. File ▸ Save As → JPEG, quality 8 to 10 (0–12 scale), into l19-processed/; then close the file
    Press Stop. Show the six recorded steps (Convert to Profile, Fit Image, Place, Flatten, Save, Close).
  4. Demo: test on one file (3m). Open l19-batch-02.jpg (a portrait file on purpose), select the Action, press Play. Say: "Always test on an odd file before trusting a batch. If a step misbehaves, delete just that step and re-record it."

Say: "Without this tick, Batch saves wherever you saved during recording, not where you told it." Also tick Suppress color profile warnings and set Errors to Log Errors to File so one bad file does not stop the run. OK, and watch.
  6. Demo: Image Processor & droplet (3m). File ▸ Scripts ▸ Image Processor: a folder to sRGB JPGs with no recording. Lab: Record & Batch (40 min)

Learners copy labs/module-6/practice/l19-input/ to their desktop with an empty l19-processed/ beside it, then: record "Web JPG 1200," test on a portrait file, batch the folder, and create a droplet. Checklist in the workbook. Fast finishers: a second Action "Print JPG 2000" and a batch comparison; or the Image Processor on the same folder.

Circulate for: Record actually pressed (the dot is red) before the first step; Fit Image, not Image Size; Flatten before Save As; Override ticked in Batch; every output in l19-processed/ at 1200 px on the long edge with the watermark; the input folder unchanged.

Beginner mistakes to pre-empt
  • Forgetting to press Stop, so browsing and fixing mistakes get recorded too. Watch the panel: if the dot is red, it is recording.
  • Recording Image Size with a fixed width. Portrait outputs come out 1200 px wide and very tall. Replace the step with Fit Image.
  • Save As shows no JPEG option. The watermark layer is still there. Flatten first (the step must be in the Action).
  • Batch output lands in the wrong folder or overwrites. Override Action "Save As" Commands was not ticked.
  • Batch stops on one file. An unexpected color mode or a missing profile. Suppress warnings, log errors to a file, and look at the log.
  • Pointing Destination at the input folder. Originals must stay untouched: always a separate processed folder.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) A recording of steps you can replay; Q2 C) Fit Image fits the long edge on any orientation; Q3 A) Tick Override Action "Save As" Commands; Q4 C) Layer ▸ Flatten Image; Q5 B) Image Processor; Q6 A) An Action saved as a drag-and-drop app. Journal prompt: three repetitive tasks in your own workflow an Action could eat.

Differentiation
  • Fast: second Action (2000 px print JPG), droplet on the desktop, Image Processor comparison ("when is it quicker than a full Action?"). Going further: add a Curves grade step to an Action.

In pairs, each learner shows the one-line brief, moodboard (3 to 5 colors, one or two fonts, a thumbnail layout), skill map (moves tagged by module), and current file. The partner answers the lesson's question: "What do you expect this to look like?" If the answer matches the brief, the plan communicates.

Triage as you walk: Red = no plan or under 30% built; yellow = built but stuck on a specific skill; green = finishing and polishing. Write initials in three columns on your sheet. This list drives the first circulation round.

Watch-outs
  • No brief chosen. Give a 60-second decision: point at the table, set a timer, commit. Then scope it down with a real constraint from the lesson (square format, two-color palette, a time limit).
  • A "back pocket" of alternatives. The lesson says commit. Two half-built pieces present worse than one finished one.
  • Document set up wrong for the destination. Print: Print preset, real-world size, 300 ppi (convert to CMYK for the printer at export, Module 5). Web/social: pixel size for the platform, RGB. Check this during the review; it is cheap to fix now and painful later.
  • Moodboard as a copy list. References are for feeling and direction, not things to copy.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 A) What is it for, who is it for, what does done look like; Q2 B) the gig poster sentence; Q3 A) palette, one or two fonts, rough layout; Q4 B) Print preset, real-world size, 300 ppi; Q5 B) one notch, not ten. Journal: reread the Module 0 entry; does the chosen brief connect to it?

Differentiation
  • Confident learners: add a real constraint (square format, a two-color palette, a 30-minute limit on one stage) or a client-style audience.
  • Hesitant learners: steer to old photo restoration (use l21-backup-old-photo.jpg) or a retouched portrait: clear "done," familiar skills, no blank canvas.
L21

Studio Session: Build It Well

homework + 90 min studio
Objectives
  • Build in the right order: block in big, refine small
  • Keep a complex file organized with named, grouped, color-labeled layers
  • Protect work with snapshots and versioned saves
  • Use a habit of stepping back to judge the whole piece
Studio launch (5 min, at the projector)
  1. The studio loop. Block in → refine big to small → step back → refine again → finish and tidy. Say: "Stepping back is a step, not a break."
  2. File habits, shown once. Select layers, Ctrl/⌘ + G to group, double-click the group name to rename it (BACKGROUND, SUBJECT, TYPE, GRADE), right-click the group's eye icon to pick a color label. In Window ▸ History, click the camera icon (Create New Snapshot), or Alt/⌥-click it to name the snapshot. Say: "History, snapshots included, clears when you close the file. Save versions too: capstone_v1.psd, _v2."
  3. Help flags. Every learner puts a card on top of the monitor: green = working, yellow = question when you are free, red = blocked. Say: "Red means you have been stuck for five minutes. Do not sit on a red for longer than that."
  4. Timed cues. Announce them now so nobody is surprised: step-back cue, "stop adding, start finishing" cue, and the export deadline.
Circulation & check-in protocol (75 min working + 10 min ship prep)
  1. Round 1 (0:25–0:45): reds first. Visit every red from the triage list, 3 minutes max each. Goal: a scoped-down, finishable target for today ("Drop the second figure. Finish the sky, the subject, and the title"). Then yellows.
  2. Round 2 (0:45–1:20): everyone. A 2 to 3 minute check-in with each learner using the same four prompts: "Show me your brief." · "What should I see first?" (focal point) · "What is your next move?" · file health: are layers named and grouped, is there a snapshot or a saved version? Leave each learner with one concrete next step.
  3. Answer yellow and red flags between check-ins. Keep hands off the mouse: guide verbally ("Open the History panel. Which snapshot is before the problem?"). If three or more learners are stuck on the same skill, call a 5-minute clinic at the projector for those learners only.
  4. Whole-room cue at 0:55: step back. "Zoom to fit, squint, then View ▸ Flip Horizontal. It flips only the view; choose it again to flip back. Does it still serve the brief? Is the focal point winning?"
  5. Whole-room cue at 1:20: stop adding, start finishing. "No new elements now. Unifying grade over everything, a 100% pass for stray artifacts, name and group anything unnamed, save capstone_final.psd."
  6. Ship prep (1:40–1:50). Every learner exports a delivery file (JPG or PNG in sRGB for screen; print pieces also export a screen JPG for the projector), names it firstname-capstone.jpg, and copies it to the shared folder. Then the two-sentence statement in the workbook. Optional if time allows: place the export into a mockup (see L22).

Track it: tick each learner's name when they have had a Round 2 check-in and again when their file lands in the shared folder. At 1:45, personally chase anyone without a tick.

Watch-outs
  • Polishing an eyelash before the layout works. Ask "Is the composition decided?" If not, back to block-in.
  • "Layer 12 copy 3." Name as you go, not at the end. Make naming and grouping a condition of your check-in: you will help once the stack is named.
  • The mid-project dip. "It looks worse than before" is normal; it is the middle. Jump to an earlier snapshot or version, step back, reread the brief.
  • Huge, slow file. Save a full layered version first, then merge or flatten finished sections in a working copy; Edit ▸ Purge if history is eating memory. (Converting groups to Smart Objects tidies the stack but usually does not shrink the file.)
  • New generative experiments in class. Credits and network are not guaranteed; steer to finishing with offline tools.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) block in, then refine big to small; Q2 A) right-click the group's eye icon and pick a color; Q3 B) History clears on close, so also save versions; Q4 B) it flips only the view; Q5 A) save a full layered version, then merge in a working copy. Journal: which skill did the capstone lean on most, and which one needs more practice?

Differentiation
  • Ahead of schedule: the "Going further": pick a snapshot, use the History panel's Create new document from current state button, and export that as a process image. Or build a second context (phone screen as well as a framed print).
  • Behind: agree a "minimum presentable" version: the focal element finished, one unifying grade, clean edges at 100%. Unfinished side elements get hidden, not rushed.
L22

Present & Ship

50 min + 8 min close
Objectives
  • Export the piece correctly for its destination
  • Present it in context with mockups so it reads as finished work
  • Write a short artist statement and critique the piece with a rubric
  • Build a portfolio entry and know where to grow next
Framing the critique (2 min, before the first presenter)
  1. Say: "A piece, not a file. Presenting, critiquing, and shipping are what turn pixels into work people respond to. Done is a decision, and today everyone decides."
  2. Norms on the board: critique the work, not the person · be specific (point at the pixels) · judge against the brief, not your taste · the presenter listens and says thank you, no defending · kind is not the same as vague.
  3. Hand out the rubric cards (the L22 table): Focal point · Light & color · Edges & masks · Type (if any) · Believability · The squint test.
Presentation & structured peer critique protocol (about 4 min per learner)
  1. Present (90 s). The presenter shows the final (in its mockup if they made one), reads the two-sentence statement (what it is and who it is for; one interesting decision and why), and shows one process image or before/after if they have it.
  2. Squint test (15 s). The whole room squints at the projector. You ask: "What do you see first?" Two or three people call it out. Does it match the presenter's intended focal point?
  3. Two reviewers (90 s). Assigned in advance: the next two people in the presentation order. Each opens with the lesson's outside-eyes question ("The first thing I notice is… and what confused me was…"), then picks one rubric row and gives one glow (what works, specifically) and one grow (phrased "I wonder if…" or "What if you tried…").
  4. Presenter notes (15 s). The presenter writes the grow in the workbook's critique box and says thank you. Instructor adds at most one sentence, and only if the reviewers missed something important.

Group size: 50 minutes fits about 11 learners at 4 minutes each with transitions. For 12 or more, split into pods of 4 to 5 around a laptop, with a printed rubric and a pod timekeeper; run the same protocol in parallel for 30 minutes while you float, then each pod nominates one piece for a whole-room showcase (15 minutes). For very small groups (under 6), give each presenter 7 to 8 minutes and let everyone review.

Watch-outs
  • Apologizing before showing ("It's not finished, I ran out of time…"). Coach it out in advance: start with the statement, not the excuse.
  • Reviewers who only say "I love it." Ask "Which rubric row, and what exactly on screen?"
  • Harsh or taste-based critique. Redirect to the brief: "Does it do what the brief says for that audience?"
  • The layered .psd sent as the delivery file. The master stays safe; the delivery is JPG or PNG in sRGB for screen, or a print PDF at 300 ppi in CMYK with bleed to the printer's spec.
  • Undisclosed generative content. Ask presenters to mention generative edits in the statement where honesty matters; Content Credentials can travel with the file.
Course close (8 min)
  1. Where to go next. Show the four paths: photo retoucher (Modules 2–3), compositor / matte artist (Modules 1 and 4), graphic / poster designer (Module 5, pair with Illustrator), speed & production (Module 6). Depth beats breadth. Point them at the Recipes library for "how do I do X again?"
  2. The one idea to keep. Say: "Work non-destructively, and decide what the viewer should see first. Nearly everything in this course is a variation on those two ideas."
  3. Final journal. Assign the final prompt to finish at home: reread the very first Module 0 entry and answer it from the other side.
  4. Celebrate. Thank them for finishing; courses are easy to start and hard to finish. Applause for every piece.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) JPG or PNG in sRGB, keeping the .psd master; Q2 A) double-click the Smart Object thumbnail, place the art in the .psb tab, save and close; Q3 B) what it is and who it is for, then one decision and why; Q4 A) shadows agree on one light, no telltale AI artifacts; Q5 B) use the rubric to decide, then ship.

Differentiation
  • Confident presenters: ask them to show two contexts (a wall frame and a phone) and a process strip, and to name the one grow they already plan to act on.
  • Anxious presenters: let them present from their seat, or have you read the statement while they drive the screen. Seat them mid-order, never first or last.

If the Room Arrives Behind

Plan B for learners who did not build at home

Some learners will arrive with a plan but little built, or with nothing. Do not let them derail the session. Scope to one image, one focal point. Offer a ready-made brief: restore l21-backup-old-photo.jpg, or build a product hero shot from l21-backup-product.jpg and l21-backup-backdrop.jpg. They still present, framed honestly as a "studio sketch," and receive the same critique. Offer a follow-up: email the finished piece within a week for written feedback. If more than half the room is behind, shorten each presentation to 3 minutes and give the saved time to the studio block.

Closing the Course

After Session 8

Collect delivery files (with permission) for a class gallery. Send each learner their critique notes and the Recipes link. Recap the whole arc: fix and export one photo → layers and masks → light and color → retouching → compositing → design and type → AI and speed → a finished, presented portfolio piece. When they started Module 0, that list was a wish list. Now it describes their skills.
