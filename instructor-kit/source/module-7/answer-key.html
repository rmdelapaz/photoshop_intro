Module 7: Answers & Capstone Notes

Self-check answers with explanations, plus reference notes for the three capstone stages: what a solid plan, build, and presentation look like, and the common weak results and why they happen.

The participant workbook's self-checks use the same question numbers as this key, so a learner's "L21 Q3" is always this key's L21 Q3.

L20 · Choose Your Brief & Plan

Q1
Which three questions should you answer before opening Photoshop?
A) What is it for? Who is it for? What does "done" look like?
Why: Purpose sets every later decision; the audience shapes tone and the destination sets size, resolution, and color mode; defining "done" up front tells you when to stop. Tools, fonts, and layer counts are downstream choices.
Q2
Which of these is a complete one-line brief?
B) "A gig poster for a local band, to be seen printed on a wall, that feels loud, retro, and warm."
Why: It fills every slot of the template: what (gig poster), who (a local band's audience), where (printed on a wall), and three adjectives. A is a vague wish; C is document settings, not a brief.
Q3
What belongs on a quick moodboard?
A) A palette of 3 to 5 colors, one or two fonts, and a rough layout sketch
Why: The moodboard's only job is to make you decide mood, palette, and layout before the canvas does. Rough is fine; scribbles count.
Q4
Your piece is a poster to be printed. How do you set up the document?
B) The Print preset at real-world size, 300 ppi, converting to CMYK for the printer at export
Why: Print needs real-world size at 300 ppi. The course designs in RGB and converts to the printer's CMYK profile at export (Module 5). A 72 ppi web document would print soft; "fix it later" means upscaling or rebuilding.
Q5
How ambitious should your capstone be?
B) One notch beyond what you have done, not ten
Why: A finished, polished piece at 80% of your dream idea beats an abandoned masterpiece. Repeating something already made teaches little; the epic version can come next.
Reference: a solid plan

Looks like: a one-line brief with all four slots filled; a clear definition of "done"; 5 to 10 references with a note on what each contributes; 3 to 5 palette colors; one or two fonts; a thumbnail layout that shows the focal point, title, and supporting info; a skill map of specific moves tagged by module (e.g. "cut out subject (M4), match light (M4), type hierarchy (M5), grade (M2)"); and a document whose size, resolution, and color mode match the destination. The partner's answer to "What do you expect this to look like?" roughly matches the brief.

Common weak plans: Adjectives missing or generic ("nice, cool, good"): nothing to steer decisions; ask "how should a viewer feel?" Three fonts or eight colors: Module 5's rule is one or two fonts; trim. Skill map says "Photoshop stuff": ask for moves, not topics. Scope for a series (three posters, a full brand system): scale to one piece plus, at most, one variation. Web-sized document for a print piece: rebuild the document now, before any work goes into it.

L21 · Studio Session: Build It Well

Q1
What is the right build order for a big piece?
B) Block in the composition roughly, then refine big to small
Why: Time spent perfecting an eyelash before the composition is decided is time you will throw away. Only once the big shapes work do details deserve attention.
Q2
How do you give a layer group a color label?
A) Right-click the group's eye (visibility) icon and pick a color
Why: That is the lesson's method. Renaming is done by double-clicking the group name; double-clicking a thumbnail does something else (for example, opens a Smart Object's contents).
Q3
Which statement about History snapshots is true?
B) History clears when you close the file, snapshots included, so you should also save versioned files
Why: Snapshots are session-only restore points. Versioned saves (capstone_v1.psd, _v2) protect you across sessions and crashes.
Q4
What does View ▸ Flip Horizontal do?
B) Flips only the view, so you see the piece with fresh eyes
Why: It changes only how the canvas is displayed, so there is nothing to undo; choose it again to flip back. Fresh eyes catch imbalances familiar eyes miss.
Q5
Your capstone file is huge and slow. What is the safe first move?
A) Save a full layered version, then merge or flatten finished sections in a working copy
Why: You keep an editable master and gain speed in the copy. Flattening the master destroys editability. Converting groups to Smart Objects tidies the stack but usually does not shrink the file, because the layers are kept inside. Edit ▸ Purge also helps if history is eating memory.
Reference: a well-built capstone file

Looks like: a capstone_final.psd master plus at least one earlier version on disk; top-level groups named by role (BACKGROUND, SUBJECT, TYPE, GRADE or similar) and color-labeled; no "Layer 12 copy 3"; adjustments on adjustment layers, placed photos as Smart Objects, and edits controlled by masks; a unifying grade at the top of the stack. The image meets the strong-piece rubric: a clear focal point, consistent light and color, clean edges and masks at 100%, deliberate type (if any), and no distracting artifacts.

Brief-specific checks: Poster: one dominant title, clear hierarchy, type inside the trim. Surreal composite: shadows and light direction agree; grain and color match across elements. Retouched portrait: skin texture kept (heal, don't erase), no plastic look, dodge & burn subtle. Restoration: scratches and dust gone without smudged detail; tone and color balanced; the result still looks like the original era. Product hero shot: clean cutout, a believable contact shadow, a background that supports rather than competes. Brand mini-kit: a logo lockup built from shapes and type; social pieces share fonts, palette, and layout via Smart Objects.

Common weak results: Flat, unnamed stack: organized "later" (never). Painted-in edits on the photo layer: destructive habits returned under pressure; ask them to rebuild the one key edit non-destructively. Halo or fringe around a cutout: skipped the 100% pass. Two focal points competing: no step-back; squint test reveals it. Elements with different light: no match or unifying grade. Over-scoped and half-finished: agree a minimum presentable version and hide unfinished elements.

L22 · Present & Ship

Q1
Your piece will be shown on screens. What do you deliver?
B) A JPG or PNG in sRGB, keeping the layered .psd master safe
Why: Screens need a flat file in sRGB (PNG if transparency is needed). The .psd is the editable master, never the delivery. A CMYK PDF with bleed is the print delivery, to the printer's spec.
Q2
How do you put your art into a mockup template?
A) Double-click the Smart Object layer's thumbnail, place your art in the .psb tab, save, and close it
Why: Mockups are built around a Smart Object. Editing its contents and saving updates the mockup, which warps the art to fit the scene. Pasting on top loses the warp and lighting; flattening destroys the template.
Q3
What goes in the two-sentence artist statement?
B) What it is and who it is for, then one interesting decision you made and why
Why: Sentence one orients the viewer; sentence two shows your thinking. Tool lists bore viewers, and apologies undercut the work before anyone has seen it.
Q4
The rubric's "Believability" row asks…
A) Do shadows agree on one light? Any telltale AI artifacts?
Why: Believability is about whether the image could be real. Font count belongs to the Type row; file size is not a rubric item.
Q5
"Done is a decision" means…
B) Use the rubric to decide the piece is finished, then ship it
Why: No piece is ever perfect. The rubric lets you call it finished with confidence; shipping a good piece today beats polishing a slightly better one forever.
Reference: a shipped capstone

Looks like: a delivery file named clearly and sized for its destination (sRGB JPG/PNG for screen; for print, a 300 ppi CMYK PDF with bleed to the printer's spec, plus a screen JPG for the class projector); the art shown in at least one context mockup with a soft shadow; a before/after or process image; a two-sentence statement in the "what and who, then one decision and why" pattern; the layered master saved separately. Generative content is disclosed where honesty matters.

Common weak results: Delivered the .psd or a CMYK JPG for screen (colors look off): re-export in sRGB. Art pasted flat over the mockup: did not edit the Smart Object, so no warp or lighting. Mockup without a shadow: looks pasted on. Statement that lists tools or apologizes: rewrite in the two-sentence pattern. Rubric self-score all "strong" with visible halos: ask them to zoom to 100% on the edges row.

Critique quality: what good peer feedback sounds like

Strong: "The first thing I notice is the red title. What confused me was the second figure on the left. On Light & color: the warm grade really sells the summer-night feel (glow). I wonder if the figure's shadow could fall to the right like the others (grow)." Weak: "I love it." (not specific) · "I'd have made it blue." (taste, not the brief) · "The whole thing doesn't work." (not actionable). Coach weak feedback toward a rubric row and a pointable spot on screen.
