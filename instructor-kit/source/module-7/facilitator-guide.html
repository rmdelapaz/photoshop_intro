Module 7: Capstone

How to run the final session: one self-directed portfolio piece, taken from brief to presented work. There are no new tools. Your job shifts from demonstrator to studio coach: protect learners' time, unblock them fast, keep scope realistic, and run a presentation and critique that is honest, kind, and specific.

3 lessons Session 8 · 3 hrs incl. break Lessons L20–L22 Format studio + critique Most of the build is homework

How to Use This Guide

  • The capstone spans two sessions. It is launched at the end of Session 7 (Module 6) as homework: learners read L20 and plan, then read L21 and build most of the piece at home. Session 8 is a quick plan review, a studio block for finishing and getting help, and presentations with structured peer critique.
  • Say this plainly, twice (at the Session 7 launch and in any midweek message): "Session 8 is not enough time to build a capstone from scratch. Arrive with your piece about 70 to 80% done. The studio block is for finishing, fixing, and getting help."
  • Teal = hands-on, amber = watch-outs. In this module the risks are scope (too ambitious), stalling (no plan), and file chaos (unnamed layers, no saves).
  • Quiz answers below match the workbook's Self-checks. L20 and L21 quizzes are homework; L22's is done after the session or skimmed during the close.
Generative features in the capstone

Some briefs (product hero shot, surreal composite) invite Generative Fill or Expand. These need internet, an Adobe sign-in, and generative credits. Tell learners to do generative steps at home, before Session 8, and never to plan a piece that depends on generating in class. If credits run out, the offline equivalents from Module 6 (Remove tool, Content-Aware Fill, Crop outward with Fill: Content-Aware Fill) plus compositing skills from Module 4 cover almost every brief. Any generative content should be disclosed in the artist statement where honesty matters.

Before Session 8

  • End of Session 7: launch the homework (see the Module 6 guide's Closing). Show the six briefs, the one-line brief template, and the "70 to 80% done" expectation.
  • Midweek (optional but powerful): ask learners to send you their one-line brief. Reply with one sentence: a green light, or a scope trim ("Great. Make it one poster, not a series of three").
  • Room prep: a shared folder (or USB drive) for delivery files, the projector machine able to open JPG/PNG/PDF, a visible timer, sticky notes or cards in three colors for help flags, and printed rubric cards (the L22 table) for reviewers.
  • Presentation order: draft it before class and put it on the board, so no one spends the studio block dreading "who goes first." Put a confident learner first.

Lesson Plans

L20

Choose Your Brief & Plan

homework + 20 min review
Objectives
  • Choose a capstone brief that plays to their strengths and interests
  • Define the goal, audience, and constraints before touching Photoshop
  • Gather references and build a quick moodboard
  • Map the skills and layer strategy the piece will need
Launching it (last minutes of Session 7)
  1. Brief before brush. Say: "Before you open Photoshop, answer three questions on paper: What is it for? Who is it for? What does done look like? People who feel they have no eye for design usually skipped this step."
  2. The six briefs. Show the L20 table: movie/event poster (Modules 4–5), surreal composite (Module 4), retouched portrait (Modules 2–3), old photo restoration (Modules 1–3), product hero shot (Modules 4 and 6), brand mini-kit (Modules 5–6). Say: "Pick the one that makes you slightly nervous in a good way."
  3. The template. Write it on the board: "A [what] for [who], to be seen [where], that feels [three adjectives]." Read the lesson's example: "A gig poster for a local band, to be seen printed on a wall, that feels loud, retro, and warm."
  4. Calibrate ambition. Say: "One notch of stretch, not ten. A finished, polished piece at 80% of your dream idea beats an abandoned masterpiece every time."
Plan review in Session 8 (20 min)

In pairs, each learner shows the one-line brief, moodboard (3 to 5 colors, one or two fonts, a thumbnail layout), skill map (moves tagged by module), and current file. The partner answers the lesson's question: "What do you expect this to look like?" If the answer matches the brief, the plan communicates.

Triage as you walk: Red = no plan or under 30% built; yellow = built but stuck on a specific skill; green = finishing and polishing. Write initials in three columns on your sheet. This list drives the first circulation round.

Watch-outs
  • No brief chosen. Give a 60-second decision: point at the table, set a timer, commit. Then scope it down with a real constraint from the lesson (square format, two-color palette, a time limit).
  • A "back pocket" of alternatives. The lesson says commit. Two half-built pieces present worse than one finished one.
  • Document set up wrong for the destination. Print: Print preset, real-world size, 300 ppi (convert to CMYK for the printer at export, Module 5). Web/social: pixel size for the platform, RGB. Check this during the review; it is cheap to fix now and painful later.
  • Moodboard as a copy list. References are for feeling and direction, not things to copy.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 A) What is it for, who is it for, what does done look like; Q2 B) the gig poster sentence; Q3 A) palette, one or two fonts, rough layout; Q4 B) Print preset, real-world size, 300 ppi; Q5 B) one notch, not ten. Journal: reread the Module 0 entry; does the chosen brief connect to it?

Differentiation
  • Confident learners: add a real constraint (square format, a two-color palette, a 30-minute limit on one stage) or a client-style audience.
  • Hesitant learners: steer to old photo restoration (use l21-backup-old-photo.jpg) or a retouched portrait: clear "done," familiar skills, no blank canvas.
L21

Studio Session: Build It Well

homework + 90 min studio
Objectives
  • Build in the right order: block in big, refine small
  • Keep a complex file organized with named, grouped, color-labeled layers
  • Protect work with snapshots and versioned saves
  • Use a habit of stepping back to judge the whole piece
Studio launch (5 min, at the projector)
  1. The studio loop. Block in → refine big to small → step back → refine again → finish and tidy. Say: "Stepping back is a step, not a break."
  2. File habits, shown once. Select layers, Ctrl/⌘ + G to group, double-click the group name to rename it (BACKGROUND, SUBJECT, TYPE, GRADE), right-click the group's eye icon to pick a color label. In Window ▸ History, click the camera icon (Create New Snapshot), or Alt/⌥-click it to name the snapshot. Say: "History, snapshots included, clears when you close the file. Save versions too: capstone_v1.psd, _v2."
  3. Help flags. Every learner puts a card on top of the monitor: green = working, yellow = question when you are free, red = blocked. Say: "Red means you have been stuck for five minutes. Do not sit on a red for longer than that."
  4. Timed cues. Announce them now so nobody is surprised: step-back cue, "stop adding, start finishing" cue, and the export deadline.
Circulation & check-in protocol (75 min working + 10 min ship prep)
  1. Round 1 (0:25–0:45): reds first. Visit every red from the triage list, 3 minutes max each. Goal: a scoped-down, finishable target for today ("Drop the second figure. Finish the sky, the subject, and the title"). Then yellows.
  2. Round 2 (0:45–1:20): everyone. A 2 to 3 minute check-in with each learner using the same four prompts: "Show me your brief." · "What should I see first?" (focal point) · "What is your next move?" · file health: are layers named and grouped, is there a snapshot or a saved version? Leave each learner with one concrete next step.
  3. Answer yellow and red flags between check-ins. Keep hands off the mouse: guide verbally ("Open the History panel. Which snapshot is before the problem?"). If three or more learners are stuck on the same skill, call a 5-minute clinic at the projector for those learners only.
  4. Whole-room cue at 0:55: step back. "Zoom to fit, squint, then View ▸ Flip Horizontal. It flips only the view; choose it again to flip back. Does it still serve the brief? Is the focal point winning?"
  5. Whole-room cue at 1:20: stop adding, start finishing. "No new elements now. Unifying grade over everything, a 100% pass for stray artifacts, name and group anything unnamed, save capstone_final.psd."
  6. Ship prep (1:40–1:50). Every learner exports a delivery file (JPG or PNG in sRGB for screen; print pieces also export a screen JPG for the projector), names it firstname-capstone.jpg, and copies it to the shared folder. Then the two-sentence statement in the workbook. Optional if time allows: place the export into a mockup (see L22).

Track it: tick each learner's name when they have had a Round 2 check-in and again when their file lands in the shared folder. At 1:45, personally chase anyone without a tick.

Watch-outs
  • Polishing an eyelash before the layout works. Ask "Is the composition decided?" If not, back to block-in.
  • "Layer 12 copy 3." Name as you go, not at the end. Make naming and grouping a condition of your check-in: you will help once the stack is named.
  • The mid-project dip. "It looks worse than before" is normal; it is the middle. Jump to an earlier snapshot or version, step back, reread the brief.
  • Huge, slow file. Save a full layered version first, then merge or flatten finished sections in a working copy; Edit ▸ Purge if history is eating memory. (Converting groups to Smart Objects tidies the stack but usually does not shrink the file.)
  • New generative experiments in class. Credits and network are not guaranteed; steer to finishing with offline tools.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) block in, then refine big to small; Q2 A) right-click the group's eye icon and pick a color; Q3 B) History clears on close, so also save versions; Q4 B) it flips only the view; Q5 A) save a full layered version, then merge in a working copy. Journal: which skill did the capstone lean on most, and which one needs more practice?

Differentiation
  • Ahead of schedule: the "Going further": pick a snapshot, use the History panel's Create new document from current state button, and export that as a process image. Or build a second context (phone screen as well as a framed print).
  • Behind: agree a "minimum presentable" version: the focal element finished, one unifying grade, clean edges at 100%. Unfinished side elements get hidden, not rushed.
L22

Present & Ship

50 min + 8 min close
Objectives
  • Export the piece correctly for its destination
  • Present it in context with mockups so it reads as finished work
  • Write a short artist statement and critique the piece with a rubric
  • Build a portfolio entry and know where to grow next
Framing the critique (2 min, before the first presenter)
  1. Say: "A piece, not a file. Presenting, critiquing, and shipping are what turn pixels into work people respond to. Done is a decision, and today everyone decides."
  2. Norms on the board: critique the work, not the person · be specific (point at the pixels) · judge against the brief, not your taste · the presenter listens and says thank you, no defending · kind is not the same as vague.
  3. Hand out the rubric cards (the L22 table): Focal point · Light & color · Edges & masks · Type (if any) · Believability · The squint test.
Presentation & structured peer critique protocol (about 4 min per learner)
  1. Present (90 s). The presenter shows the final (in its mockup if they made one), reads the two-sentence statement (what it is and who it is for; one interesting decision and why), and shows one process image or before/after if they have it.
  2. Squint test (15 s). The whole room squints at the projector. You ask: "What do you see first?" Two or three people call it out. Does it match the presenter's intended focal point?
  3. Two reviewers (90 s). Assigned in advance: the next two people in the presentation order. Each opens with the lesson's outside-eyes question ("The first thing I notice is… and what confused me was…"), then picks one rubric row and gives one glow (what works, specifically) and one grow (phrased "I wonder if…" or "What if you tried…").
  4. Presenter notes (15 s). The presenter writes the grow in the workbook's critique box and says thank you. Instructor adds at most one sentence, and only if the reviewers missed something important.

Group size: 50 minutes fits about 11 learners at 4 minutes each with transitions. For 12 or more, split into pods of 4 to 5 around a laptop, with a printed rubric and a pod timekeeper; run the same protocol in parallel for 30 minutes while you float, then each pod nominates one piece for a whole-room showcase (15 minutes). For very small groups (under 6), give each presenter 7 to 8 minutes and let everyone review.

Watch-outs
  • Apologizing before showing ("It's not finished, I ran out of time…"). Coach it out in advance: start with the statement, not the excuse.
  • Reviewers who only say "I love it." Ask "Which rubric row, and what exactly on screen?"
  • Harsh or taste-based critique. Redirect to the brief: "Does it do what the brief says for that audience?"
  • The layered .psd sent as the delivery file. The master stays safe; the delivery is JPG or PNG in sRGB for screen, or a print PDF at 300 ppi in CMYK with bleed to the printer's spec.
  • Undisclosed generative content. Ask presenters to mention generative edits in the statement where honesty matters; Content Credentials can travel with the file.
Course close (8 min)
  1. Where to go next. Show the four paths: photo retoucher (Modules 2–3), compositor / matte artist (Modules 1 and 4), graphic / poster designer (Module 5, pair with Illustrator), speed & production (Module 6). Depth beats breadth. Point them at the Recipes library for "how do I do X again?"
  2. The one idea to keep. Say: "Work non-destructively, and decide what the viewer should see first. Nearly everything in this course is a variation on those two ideas."
  3. Final journal. Assign the final prompt to finish at home: reread the very first Module 0 entry and answer it from the other side.
  4. Celebrate. Thank them for finishing; courses are easy to start and hard to finish. Applause for every piece.
Check for understanding (workbook Self-check)

Answers: Q1 B) JPG or PNG in sRGB, keeping the .psd master; Q2 A) double-click the Smart Object thumbnail, place the art in the .psb tab, save and close; Q3 B) what it is and who it is for, then one decision and why; Q4 A) shadows agree on one light, no telltale AI artifacts; Q5 B) use the rubric to decide, then ship.

Differentiation
  • Confident presenters: ask them to show two contexts (a wall frame and a phone) and a process strip, and to name the one grow they already plan to act on.
  • Anxious presenters: let them present from their seat, or have you read the statement while they drive the screen. Seat them mid-order, never first or last.

If the Room Arrives Behind

Plan B for learners who did not build at home

Some learners will arrive with a plan but little built, or with nothing. Do not let them derail the session. Scope to one image, one focal point. Offer a ready-made brief: restore l21-backup-old-photo.jpg, or build a product hero shot from l21-backup-product.jpg and l21-backup-backdrop.jpg. They still present, framed honestly as a "studio sketch," and receive the same critique. Offer a follow-up: email the finished piece within a week for written feedback. If more than half the room is behind, shorten each presentation to 3 minutes and give the saved time to the studio block.

Closing the Course

After Session 8

Collect delivery files (with permission) for a class gallery. Send each learner their critique notes and the Recipes link. Recap the whole arc: fix and export one photo → layers and masks → light and color → retouching → compositing → design and type → AI and speed → a finished, presented portfolio piece. When they started Module 0, that list was a wish list. Now it describes their skills.
