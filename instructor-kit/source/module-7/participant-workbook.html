Photoshop Workbook · Module 7

Capstone

No new tools, only you and everything you have learned. This module is one self-directed project, taken from idea to presented portfolio piece: plan it, build it well, then present it and ship it.

Lessons 20–22 3 lessons · Session 8 Name: ____________________
Most of the capstone happens at home

Session 8 is three hours, and half of it is presentations. Plan (L20) and build most of your piece (L21) before Session 8, and arrive about 70 to 80% done. In class, the studio block is for finishing, fixing, and getting help.

How to use this workbook

L20 is a planning worksheet: fill it in before you open Photoshop. L21 is your studio log. L22 holds your artist statement, your self-critique, and the feedback you receive. Each lesson also has a Self-check and a Journal prompt: key concepts · what clicked · questions · ideas to try · your progress and feelings.

L20

45 min planning

Step 1: Write the one-line brief (5 min)

"A [what] for [who], to be seen [where], that feels [three adjectives]."

Example: "A gig poster for a local band, to be seen printed on a wall, that feels loud, retro, and warm."

What does "done" look like for this piece? (How will you know when to stop?)

Step 2: Gather references (10 min)

Collect 5 to 10 images that capture the feeling you want: color, composition, mood, type. Screenshot them into one board. You are gathering feeling and direction, not things to copy. What do you like about each?

Fonts (one or two, Module 5's rule):

Layout thumbnail: where do the focal point, title, and supporting info sit? A scribble is enough.

Step 4: Map the skills (5 min)

List the moves your piece needs, tagged by module, for example: "cut out subject (M4), match light (M4), type hierarchy (M5), grade (M2)." This is your build checklist for L21. Circle any skill that feels shaky, and do a five-minute warm-up on it first.

L21

Studio Session: Build It Well

your build time
By the end you can
  • Build in the right order: block in big, refine small
  • Keep a complex file organized with named, grouped, color-labeled layers
  • Protect your work with snapshots and versioned saves
  • Use a habit of stepping back to judge the whole piece
Key ideas
  • A tidy file is a fast file. Name layers the moment they matter ("sky," "model-cutout," "title"), group by role (BACKGROUND, SUBJECT, TYPE, GRADE), and color-label the groups.
  • Build big to small. Block in the whole composition roughly; refine only once the layout works.
  • Snapshot before risky moves. Window ▸ History, camera icon (Create New Snapshot); Alt/⌥-click it to name the snapshot. History clears when you close the file, snapshots included, so also save versions: capstone_v1.psd, _v2…
  • Step back on purpose: zoom to fit, squint, and View ▸ Flip Horizontal (it flips only the view; choose it again to flip back).
Worked example: the studio session
  1. Block in: place background, subject, and space for type roughly. Select related layers, Ctrl/⌘ + G to group, double-click the group name to rename it. Right-click a group's eye icon to pick a color label. Judge the layout.
  2. Refine, module by module: cut out and mask (M1, M4), match light and color (M4), retouch (M3), grade (M2), set type (M5), add styles (M5), generative touches (M6). Stay non-destructive: adjustment layers, Smart Objects, masks. Snapshot before each risky step.
  3. Step back, throughout: does it still serve the brief? Is the focal point winning?
  4. Finish and clean up: the unifying grade over everything (M2, M4); a 100% pass for stray artifacts; every group named and labeled, dead layers deleted; save a clean capstone_final.psd master.

Watch out
  • "I'll tidy up later" becomes forty mystery layers. Naming in the moment costs seconds; retrofitting costs an hour.
  • File huge and slow? Save a full layered version first, then merge or flatten finished sections in a working copy. Edit ▸ Purge if history is eating memory.
  • Feels worse than before? Normal: it is the middle. Jump to an earlier snapshot or version, step back, and reread the brief.

L22

Present & Ship

45 min
By the end you can
  • Export your piece correctly for its destination
  • Present it in context with mockups so it reads as finished work
  • Write a short artist statement and critique your piece with a rubric
  • Build a portfolio entry and know where to grow next
Key ideas
  • Present, critique, ship. Finishing the pixels is not the finish line.
  • Keep the layered .psd master. Export a delivery copy: JPG or PNG in sRGB for screen, or a print PDF at 300 ppi, in CMYK, with bleed, to the printer's spec (Module 5).
  • Mockups are layered PSDs built around a Smart Object. Context tells the viewer how to read the work.
  • Done is a decision. The rubric helps you make that call instead of fiddling forever.
Worked example: place your art in a mockup
  1. Open a mockup PSD (Adobe Stock and many design sites offer them; check the license).
  2. Double-click the Smart Object layer's thumbnail. Its contents open in their own tab (a .psb file).
  3. Place or paste your art there and scale it to fill. Save (Ctrl/⌘ + S) and close that tab.
  4. Back in the mockup, your art appears warped to fit the scene. Add a soft shadow and a believable setting.

No mockup file? Use the plain wall scene your instructor provides: File ▸ Place Embedded your export (it arrives as a Smart Object), scale it into the frame's opening, and add a soft Drop Shadow layer style (Module 5) so it sits in the frame instead of floating on it.

Your turn: ship checklist
  • ☐ Delivery file exported correctly for its destination
  • ☐ Presented in at least one context mockup with a shadow
  • ☐ A before/after or process shot accompanies it
  • ☐ A two-sentence artist statement written
  • ☐ The layered master is saved and safe

My two-sentence statement. Sentence one: what it is and who it is for. Sentence two: one interesting decision you made and why.

Example: "A gig poster for a local retro band. I graded it warm and added film grain so it feels like a memory of a summer night."

The one idea to keep

If the tools fade from memory, keep this: work non-destructively, and decide what the viewer should see first. Skill is just reps plus reflection, and you already know how to do both.
