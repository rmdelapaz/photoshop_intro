L11 · Cut It Out Cleanly

Q1
Your cutout looked perfect on its original background but looks "pasted" on a sunset. What is the most likely cause?
B) The edge pixels still carry color from the old background (a fringe)
Why: when the subject was photographed, the edge pixels picked up color from what was behind it. The original background hides that color; a new one reveals it. Resolution (A) and blend mode (C) are not the issue.
Q2
What does Decontaminate Colors do?
B) Replaces the old background's color in the edge pixels so the edge can adopt the new scene
Why: it cleans color out of the edge rather than just choosing pixels. It is the biggest single difference between an amateur and a believable cutout.
Q3
With Decontaminate Colors ticked, which Output To setting does the lesson use?
C) New Layer with Layer Mask
Why: decontamination rewrites edge pixels, so it cannot stay fully non-destructive and Photoshop forces the result onto a new layer. The mask keeps the hiding editable, and the original layer stays underneath as a backup.
Q4
Where should you paint with the Refine Edge Brush?
B) Only on soft, wispy edges like hair and fur
Why: the brush rebuilds fine strands. Dragged across a hard edge (shoulder, jaw) it turns crisp detail to mush, and it cannot invent hair the original selection never included.
Q5
What is the lesson's "believability test" for a finished cutout?
A) Drop a saturated colored layer behind it and check whether a fringe appears
Why: if the edge stays clean against a wild color, it will stay clean against any real scene. If a fringe appears, raise the decontamination Amount. Zooming out (B) hides the problem.
Q6
What is the keyboard shortcut for Select and Mask?
C) Ctrl+Alt+R / ⌘+⌥+R
Why: Ctrl/⌘+J duplicates a layer (or copies a selection to a new layer); Ctrl/⌘+Alt/⌥+G creates a clipping mask (Lesson 12). Select and Mask is also on the Select menu and the options bar of any selection tool.
Lab reference: A Composite-Ready Cutout

A correct result: a group named SUBJECT containing a new layer with a layer mask; the original photo layer is present and hidden beneath; against a saturated Solid Color fill the hair edge shows fine, soft strands and no colored halo; solid edges (shoulders, clothing) are crisp. File saved as composite-working.psd. On l11-figure-on-green.png, the green halo around the hair and shoulders should be gone at 200% zoom.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Green (or blue) halo still visible: Decontaminate Colors not ticked, or Amount too low. Re-enter Select and Mask on the original layer and raise Amount.
  • Mushy shoulders or jaw: the Refine Edge Brush was run over hard edges. Redo the refinement, brushing hair only.
  • Whole outline soft and glowing: Smooth or Feather set high. Return them to 0 or near it.
  • Chunky, helmet-like hair: Refine Hair / Smart Radius skipped, or the initial selection missed the hair mass. Improve the selection, then brush.
  • No backup: the background was deleted rather than masked, or the original layer was thrown away. Explain why it matters: decontamination is destructive to the edge pixels.

L12 · Make It Belong: Match Light & Color

Q1
In what order does the lesson match a subject to a scene?
A) Brightness, then color, then contrast
Why: most "bad composite" feelings come from brightness alone, so fixing it first often does eighty percent of the work. Following the order keeps you from chasing your own tail.
Q2
What does clipping an adjustment layer do?
C) Limits the adjustment to the one layer directly below it
Why: an unclipped adjustment changes everything beneath it, including the background you are trying to match. Clipping uses the subject's own shape to contain the adjustment, with no mask painting needed.
Q3
Which shortcut clips the active layer to the layer below?
B) Ctrl+Alt+G / ⌘+⌥+G
Why: alternatively, hold Alt/⌥ and click the line between the two layers. Ctrl/⌘+T is Free Transform; Ctrl/⌘+Shift+N makes a new layer.
Q4
You warmed your subject evenly for a sunset and now it looks "dipped in orange." What does the lesson suggest?
B) Add a touch of the opposite (cooler) temperature to the shadow side
Why: warm light usually means cool shadows. The lit side should catch the scene's warm light; the shadow side gets a touch of the opposite temperature for realism.
Q5
Why convert the SUBJECT group to a Smart Object before scaling it?
A) So resizing never degrades it
Why: a Smart Object re-renders from its original contents each time you transform it, so repeated resizing does not soften it (scaling past its original pixel size still does). It does not block adjustments (B) or match color automatically (C).
Q6
How can you judge a scene's color temperature, according to the lesson?
C) Read the highlights and shadows on existing objects, or sample a neutral surface with the eyedropper
Why: warm highlights and cool shadows mean warm light. Sampling a neutral surface in the scene gives you the exact cast to match.
Lab reference: Match the Subject

A correct result: layer stack (top to bottom) of clipped adjustments (Curves, Color Balance, and optionally Hue/Saturation, each showing ↳), the SUBJECT Smart Object with its name underlined, and the background scene. The subject is sized for its spot in the scene with its feet on the ground. When squinting, it does not pop out as brighter than its neighbors. On l12-scene-sunset.jpg the subject's lit (right) side is warm orange-gold and its shadow side is slightly cooler; saturation is a little lower than the original cutout. Toggling the clipped layers off makes the subject look obviously pasted.

Common wrong results and why:

  • The sky or ground changed color too: an adjustment is not clipped. Clip it.
  • Subject still glows against the scene: color was adjusted but brightness never was. Go back to step 1 and squint.
  • Uniformly orange subject: warmth applied everywhere, including the shadows. Cool the shadow tones a touch in Color Balance.
  • Soft, blurry subject: it was scaled several times before being converted to a Smart Object, or scaled far beyond its original size.
  • Floating giant: scale and placement ignore perspective; compare to nearby objects and put the feet on the ground plane.

L13 · Ground It & Grade It

Q1
According to the lesson, what is the strongest single cue that a subject belongs in a scene?
B) A contact shadow where the subject meets the ground
Why: nothing real floats. The first dark sliver of shadow at the contact point is what sells contact; the cast shadow, haze, and grade add to it.
Q2
Why do the contact shadow and the cast shadow go on separate layers?
A) So you can blur the cast shadow without softening the contact point
Why: the contact shadow must stay tight and dark and is never blurred; the cast shadow gets softer as it travels, so it alone gets Gaussian Blur.
Q3
The sun in your background is high and to the right. Which way should your subject's cast shadow fall?
C) Low-left, away from the sun
Why: shadows fall away from the light. There is one sun, so every shadow in the scene must fall the same way; check the background's existing shadows and match them.
Q4
Compared with nearby things, how do distant things look in a real scene?
B) Hazier, lighter, and lower in contrast
Why: air is not perfectly clear. That atmospheric perspective is what a low-opacity haze layer recreates for a mid or far subject.
Q5
Where does the unifying grade go in the layer stack?
A) At the very top, over everything
Why: because it sits over both subject and background, it forces them to share one final look, the way a single camera would. Clipped to the subject (B) it would do the opposite: separate them.
Q6
What is the correct way to finish and deliver the composite?
B) Save the layered .psd master, then File ▸ Export ▸ Export As a JPG
Why: the master stays fully editable; Export As flattens the JPG automatically. Flattening and saving over the .psd (A) throws away every layer.
Lab reference: Ground, Depth, Grade (Module 4 deliverable)

A correct result: two shadow layers below the SUBJECT: Contact Shadow (small, darkest at the feet, unblurred) and Cast Shadow (longer, softer, blurred, falling the same way as the scene's shadows; on l12-scene-sunset.jpg and l13-scene-hard-sun.jpg that is toward the left). Optional haze layer for a distant subject. One unclipped grade (Color Lookup or Curves) plus a soft vignette at the top. A layered .psd and an exported JPG. At a squint, the subject reads as standing on the ground, not hovering in front of it.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Subject still floats: no contact shadow, or it is too light or too far from the feet.
  • Shadow points the wrong way: the learner didn't check the scene's existing shadows first.
  • A gray blob shadow: uniform opacity and softness; it should fade and soften along its length.
  • Contact point looks fuzzy: Gaussian Blur was applied to the contact layer, or both shadows were painted on one layer.
  • Shadows show on top of the subject: shadow layers were created above the SUBJECT group. Drag them below.
  • Obvious fog or heavy color cast: haze or grade too strong. Both should be subtle; if the grade is doing the heavy lifting, revisit the L12 match.
  • Only a flattened file exists: the master was flattened. Recover from the last layered save if possible; reinforce Save first, then Export As.
