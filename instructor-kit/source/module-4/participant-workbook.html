L11

Cut It Out Cleanly

45 min
By the end you can
  • Cut a subject out with Select & Mask, tuned for compositing
  • Rescue fine hair with the Refine Edge Brush
  • Kill the fringe with Decontaminate Colors
  • Output a new layer with a layer mask, ready for any scene
Key ideas
  • The background tells on you. Edge pixels picked up color from whatever was behind the subject. On a new scene, that leftover color shows as a fringe (a halo).
  • A composite cutout has two jobs: choose the right pixels, and clean the old color out of the edge.
  • Decontaminate Colors strips that fringe. It rewrites edge pixels, so Photoshop forces the result onto a new layer; keep your original as a hidden backup.
  • The Refine Edge Brush is for soft, wispy edges (hair, fur) only. On a hard edge it turns detail to mush.
Worked example: the settings that matter
  1. Select ▸ Subject, then Select ▸ Select and Mask… (Ctrl+Alt+R / ⌘+⌥+R). View: On Layers or Overlay.
  2. Refine Hair (options bar). Edge Detection: tick Smart Radius, raise Radius a few pixels.
  3. Refine Edge Brush along wispy hair. Shift Edge slightly negative. Smooth and Feather at or near 0 on hair.
  4. Output Settings: tick Decontaminate Colors, raise Amount until the halo disappears (the lesson's diagram shows 90%).
  5. Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask → OK. Group it, name the group SUBJECT, keep the original hidden below. Save as composite-working.psd.
Your turn: a composite-ready cutout

Cut out your subject (or l11-figure-on-green.png). Then run the believability test: add Layer ▸ New Fill Layer ▸ Solid Color in a wild color and drag it under the cutout. Record:

Subject I used 
Where the fringe showed before decontaminating 
Decontaminate Amount that removed it 
Test colors I tried (aim for three) 
  • ☐ Subject on its own layer with a mask (background hidden, not deleted)
  • ☐ Hair edges look soft and natural, not chopped
  • ☐ No colored fringe against a saturated test background
  • ☐ Original layer kept as a hidden backup
  • ☐ Cutout group named and saved
Watch out

Don't over-brush. The Refine Edge Brush on a shoulder or jaw turns crisp detail to mush. And it cannot invent hair the original selection never included: fix the selection first.

L12

Make It Belong: Match Light & Color

45 min
By the end you can
  • Diagnose a "pasted" look by checking brightness, color, and contrast
  • Match the subject with clipped adjustment layers
  • Pull the scene's color into the subject with Curves and Color Balance
Key ideas
  • Everything in a real scene shares the same light, like a shared accent. A pasted subject has a foreign accent.
  • One rule: match brightness first, color second, contrast third. Brightness alone often does eighty percent of the work.
  • A clipped adjustment affects only the layer directly below it. Clip with Ctrl+Alt+G / ⌘+⌥+G, or Alt/⌥-click the line between the two layers. A clipped layer shows an indent and a bent arrow (↳); the layer it clips to has its name underlined.
  • Warm light usually means cool shadows. Warm the lit side; don't dip the whole subject in orange.
Worked example: diagnosing a pasted subject

A subject shot in a cool, bright studio is dropped into a warm, hazy sunset. Ask the three questions in order:

QuestionWhat you seeClipped fix
1 · BrightnessSubject brighter than everything around itCurves: pull the curve down until it sits in the scene's range (squint to check)
2 · ColorSubject bluish; scene orangeColor Balance: add red and yellow in midtones and highlights; a touch cooler in the shadows
3 · ContrastSubject crisp and saturated; scene softCurves: flatten contrast a little; Hue/Saturation: Saturation down a few points
Your turn: match the subject

File ▸ Place Embedded your background (or l12-scene-sunset.jpg), press Enter, drag it below SUBJECT. Right-click the group ▸ Convert to Smart Object, then Ctrl/⌘+T to size it with its feet on the ground. Match in order. Before you touch a slider, write your diagnosis:

Brightness: too bright, too dark, or matched? 
Scene temperature (warm / cool / neutral) 
Scene contrast (soft and hazy / punchy and crisp) 
  • ☐ Sized and positioned believably (feet on the ground)
  • ☐ Brightness matches nearby scene elements
  • ☐ The lit side carries the scene's color; shadows are not over-warmed
  • ☐ Contrast and saturation match the scene
  • ☐ Every adjustment is clipped (↳), background untouched

Squint-and-flick test: squint at the whole image, then toggle all your clipped layers off and on. The "off" version should look obviously wrong.

L13

Ground It & Grade It

55 min
By the end you can
  • Anchor a subject with a contact shadow and a soft cast shadow
  • Add depth with atmospheric perspective (haze)
  • Tie everything together with one unifying grade
  • Save a layered master and export a finished composite
Key ideas
  • Shadow (the object): nothing real floats. A contact shadow is the strongest single cue that a subject belongs.
  • Depth (the space): distant things are hazier, lighter, and lower in contrast. A whisper of haze restores distance.
  • Unity (the whole): one grade over everything gives the image a single shared look, like one camera and lens.
  • One light, one shadow direction. If the sun is high-right, shadows fall low-left.
Worked example: the finishing moves
  1. New layer below the subject, named Contact Shadow. Soft black brush, low opacity: a small, dark pool right where the feet touch the ground. Never blur this layer.
  2. New layer just below that, named Cast Shadow. A longer, softer shadow away from the light, lighter as it travels. Blur only this layer: Filter ▸ Blur ▸ Gaussian Blur.
  3. For a mid or far subject: a low-opacity soft layer of the scene's haze color, masked to the distance.
  4. At the very top: a Color Lookup or gentle Curves for one shared mood, plus a vignette (new layer, big soft black brush on the corners, lower the opacity).
  5. File ▸ Save the layered master. File ▸ Export ▸ Export As ▸ JPG (flattened automatically).
Your turn: find the light, then finish

Before painting any shadow, study your background. Sketch the scene below: draw an arrow for where the light comes from and the direction the existing shadows fall. Then mark where your subject's contact and cast shadows will go.

  • ☐ A dark, tight contact shadow where the subject meets the ground
  • ☐ A softer, blurred cast shadow on its own layer, same direction as the scene's shadows
  • ☐ Distance reads through subtle atmosphere
  • ☐ One unifying grade over the whole stack
  • ☐ Layered master saved; finished JPG exported 🏆
Watch out

A fake-looking shadow is usually too uniform: vary it from dark and sharp at the contact point to soft and light at the tip. And if you can clearly see your haze or your grade, it's too strong. Both are whispers.

Notes

Module 4 Summary

  • A composite cutout must survive a new background; Decontaminate Colors strips the fringe.
  • A pasted look is a mismatch of brightness, color, or contrast. Fix them in that order, every adjustment clipped to the subject.
  • A contact shadow grounds the subject; haze adds depth; one grade on top unifies the whole.
  • There is only one light: every shadow falls the same way.
