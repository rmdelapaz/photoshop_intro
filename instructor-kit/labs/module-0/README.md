# Module 0 Labs: Your First Win (Session 1)

Session 1 has one lesson, **L01 Fix & Export One Photo**, run twice: a guided build on a
practice file with the instructor, then an independent pass on the learner's own photo.

```
practice/                       generated synthetic files (safe to redistribute)
  l01-tilted-horizon.jpg        guided build: crooked, dark seascape to straighten, crop, brighten
  l01-too-bright.jpg            independent pass fallback: washed-out meadow to darken + add Vibrance
```

> **Never use the course site's `images/` folder for labs.** Those photos are Adobe Stock
> licensed to the author and are not redistributable. The files in `practice/` are
> generated by script and can be copied to every learner machine.

## Practice file specs (for the generator script)

All files: sRGB JPEG, quality 90, 8-bit RGB, no EXIF. Coordinates are in pixels from the
top-left corner. "Noise" means per-pixel Gaussian noise added to all three channels, then
clipped to 0–255.

### `l01-tilted-horizon.jpg`: crooked, dark seascape

- **Size:** 2400 × 1600.
- **Scene (draw level first, then rotate):**
  - Sky: vertical gradient from RGB (70, 110, 170) at the top to RGB (190, 205, 225) at
    the horizon. Horizon at y = 820 before rotation.
  - Sun: soft disk, radius 70, center (1650, 560), color RGB (250, 235, 190), edge blurred
    (Gaussian radius 25) into the sky.
  - Sea: vertical gradient from RGB (40, 80, 120) at the horizon to RGB (15, 35, 60) at the
    bottom, with ~40 thin, lighter horizontal streaks (1–3 px tall, 60–300 px long, RGB
    (90, 130, 170), alpha 40–60%) as wave glints, denser near the horizon.
  - A small dark sailboat silhouette (triangle sail 60 px tall + 90 × 14 hull), RGB
    (25, 30, 40), sitting on the horizon at x ≈ 700. It gives learners a level reference.
- **The flaws to fix:**
  1. **Tilt:** rotate the whole scene **4° clockwise** (PIL `rotate(-4, resample=BICUBIC,
     expand=False)`) after drawing it on a canvas ~10% larger, then center-crop back to
     2400 × 1600 so no empty corners show. Clockwise means the horizon runs lower on the
     right, so learners must rotate counterclockwise to fix it.
  2. **Underexposed and flat:** after rotation, compress tones to output = 20 + input × 0.55
     (all channels) so the image looks dark and low in contrast (nothing brighter than
     about RGB 160). Brightness/Contrast should visibly help.
  3. **Distracting edge:** a dark, blurry blob (a "thumb in the frame") in the bottom-left
     corner: ellipse 380 × 300 centered at (60, 1580), RGB (35, 25, 20), Gaussian blur
     radius 40. It must sit fully inside the outer ~15% of the frame so a normal crop
     removes it.
- **Noise:** sigma 3.
- **Correct result:** cropped to remove the blob, horizon level, shadows lifted and a little
  more contrast.

### `l01-too-bright.jpg`: washed-out meadow

- **Size:** 2400 × 1600.
- **Scene:**
  - Sky (top 45%): vertical gradient RGB (175, 205, 235) → RGB (225, 235, 245), with 5–6
    soft white cloud blobs (ellipses 200–450 px wide, blur radius 30).
  - Meadow (bottom 55%): vertical gradient RGB (95, 150, 70) → RGB (60, 110, 45), with
    ~2,000 short grass strokes (lines 8–25 px long, 2 px wide, slight random angle, greens
    between RGB (50, 100, 40) and RGB (120, 170, 80)).
  - ~120 "flowers": filled circles radius 6–16 px, colors chosen randomly from red
    (200, 60, 60), yellow (230, 200, 60), purple (140, 80, 170), white (240, 240, 240),
    scattered across the meadow (bigger and sparser toward the bottom for depth).
  - One tree: trunk rectangle 40 × 260 at x ≈ 1800, RGB (80, 60, 40); canopy made of three
    overlapping circles (radius 110–150), RGB (50, 100, 50), top of the canopy crossing the
    horizon into the sky.
  - The horizon is already level (this file is about tone and color, not straightening).
- **The flaw to fix:** overexposed and washed out. After drawing: blend 45% toward white
  (output = input × 0.55 + 255 × 0.45), then reduce saturation by 35% (convert to HSV and
  scale S by 0.65, or use `ImageEnhance.Color(img).enhance(0.65)`). Blacks should sit
  around RGB 115+; nothing should be truly dark.
- **Noise:** sigma 2.
- **Correct result:** Brightness lowered a touch, gentle Vibrance boost; flowers and grass
  look richer, sky clouds regain a little shape.

## Lab steps

**L01 guided build (with the instructor, 0:55–1:45).** Open `l01-tilted-horizon.jpg` →
File ▸ Save As `myphoto-working.psd` (Save on your computer) → Crop tool (C): crop out the
blob, rotate with the bent arrow until the grid matches the horizon, Enter/Return →
Layers panel ◐ ▸ Brightness/Contrast: nudge Brightness and Contrast up → toggle the eyeball
→ File ▸ Save → File ▸ Export ▸ Export As, JPG ~80%, `myphoto-final.jpg`.

**Independent pass (2:00–2:35).** Same loop, no demo, on the learner's own photo (or
`l01-too-bright.jpg`). Lesson twist: on a too-bright photo, lower Brightness a touch, then
add a second adjustment layer ◐ ▸ Vibrance and push Vibrance up gently. Export with a new
name. Going further: swap the order of the two adjustment layers and compare.

**Definition of done:** two finished loops, each with a .psd holding at least one adjustment
layer that toggles cleanly, an exported .jpg with a new name, and the original untouched.

## Learners' own photos: what to bring

Ask learners (in the pre-class email) to copy **4–5 photos of their own** onto the laptop
they'll use, not just leave them on a phone. For Session 1 the important ones are:

1. **A photo that's a little dark or a little crooked:** a phone snapshot is perfect.
   A horizon, a doorway, or a building edge makes straightening meaningful.
2. **A photo that's too bright:** a sunny beach, snow, a washed-out sky, an overexposed
   indoor shot. This is the independent pass.

It also helps to bring the photos Session 2 needs (see `labs/module-1/README.md`): a
landscape with a pale sky, a photo with one clear subject, and a photo with a distraction.

No photos of their own? Learners can download free photos from sites such as
**Pexels** or **Unsplash**. Check the license on the site before using a photo (both
currently allow free use for this kind of practice, but terms can change), and credit the
photographer if the work will be shared publicly.

Do not hand out solutions before the lab.
