L01 · Fix & Export One Photo

Q1
What does non-destructive editing mean?
B) Making changes on layers above the photo, so the original pixels are never altered
Why: The tracing-paper idea: changes live on sheets (layers) you can toggle, tweak, or peel off. A) confuses "non-destructive" with "subtle"; a big edit on an adjustment layer is still non-destructive. C) is still editing pixels directly, just on a copy.
Q2
Why do you choose File ▸ Save As and the .psd format right after opening your photo?
B) A .psd remembers your layers so you can change your mind, and your original JPEG stays untouched
Why: This is the tracing-paper principle applied to the file itself. A .psd is usually larger than a JPEG (A is wrong), and Photoshop opens JPEGs fine (C is wrong); a JPEG simply can't store layers.
Q3
With the Crop tool active, how do you straighten a tilted horizon?
A) Move the cursor just outside a corner until it becomes a bent arrow, then drag to rotate until the grid lines match the horizon
Why: Straightening is rotating the crop grid to agree with a line that should be level. Dragging a corner inward (B) only trims edges; dragging inside the box (C) repositions the image.
Q4
You click the eyeball next to your Brightness/Contrast layer and the whole effect disappears. What does that tell you?
C) The edit lives on the adjustment layer, not in the photo itself
Why: This is "the magic moment" from the lesson: non-destructive editing you can see. Nothing failed (A), and the photo was never permanently changed (B).
Q5
What's the difference between Save and Export?
B) Save writes your layered .psd so you can keep editing; Export flattens everything into a share-ready .jpg
Why: You do both, for different reasons, every time: the .psd is the editable master, the .jpg is the copy you share. Never post the .psd; never edit on the .jpg.
Q6
You can't find the Layers panel anywhere on screen. What do you do?
A) Choose Window ▸ Layers (or press F7)
Why: Every panel lives in the Window menu. (Resetting the workspace with Window ▸ Workspace also brings panels back, but the direct fix is Window ▸ Layers.)
Lab reference: guided build (l01-tilted-horizon.jpg or own photo)

A correct result looks like: two files. myphoto-working.psd opens with two layers: the photo at the bottom and a Brightness/Contrast adjustment layer above it; toggling that layer's eyeball shows a clear before/after. myphoto-final.jpg is cropped (the dark blob in the practice file's bottom-left corner is gone), the horizon is level (within about half a degree to the eye), and shadows are more open with a little more punch. The original .jpg is unchanged on disk.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Only one layer in the .psd, but the photo is brighter. The learner used Image ▸ Adjustments (a direct, destructive edit) instead of the ◐ button in the Layers panel. Show them the ◐ button and redo Step 3.
  • Horizon tilted the other way, or still tilted. They rotated in the wrong direction or estimated by eye without lining a grid line up with the horizon. Have them rotate until one horizontal grid line sits exactly on it.
  • Crop is a perfect square or odd shape. The Crop tool's ratio menu was set from a previous use. Click Clear in the options bar and redo.
  • No .psd anywhere, or the .psd has no adjustment layer. They skipped Save As, or skipped the final File ▸ Save before exporting, so the .psd only holds the state from Step 1.
  • Original photo was overwritten. They exported with the original's name into the same folder. Lesson learned: always export as -final.jpg.
  • Washed-out, flat-gray result. Brightness pushed very high with no Contrast. "Nudge" both; this file needs a moderate lift, not the maximum.
Lab reference: independent pass (learner's own photo, or l01-too-bright.jpg)

Own photos vary, so judge the process, not a specific look: a .psd saved before editing, at least one adjustment layer that toggles cleanly, a new-name export, and an original left untouched. With the lesson's too-bright twist, a correct result looks like: a .psd with two adjustment layers above the photo (Brightness/Contrast with Brightness lowered slightly, and Vibrance with Vibrance raised gently), plus an exported .jpg. Colors look richer without neon edges; highlights have a little more detail than the original.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Garish, neon colors. Saturation was pushed instead of (or as well as) Vibrance, or Vibrance went to the maximum. Vibrance is the gentler control; "gently" is the lesson's word.
  • Only one adjustment layer. They edited the existing Brightness/Contrast layer again instead of clicking ◐ a second time. The point of the exercise is seeing two sheets of tracing paper.
  • A visibly worse result than the original. Usually a dark photo darkened further or a bright one brightened: the learner copied the demo's slider direction instead of looking at their own photo. Ask "what does this photo need?" and let them fix it on the same layer (that's the point of adjustment layers).
  • "Reordering changed nothing." A valid observation: with these two adjustments the difference is often subtle. The going-further question is about curiosity, not a specific answer.

L02 · The Layer Stack & Your First Mask

Q1
In the stack-of-glass model, what happens where a higher layer is opaque?
B) It covers what's below it
Why: "Higher layers win where they're opaque." Clear glass lets lower sheets show through; opaque paint on a higher sheet hides them. That's also why order is meaning.
Q2
You paint black on an adjustment layer's mask. What happens in that area?
A) The adjustment is hidden there, and the original shows through
Why: Black hides, white reveals. Painting on a mask never paints the photo (B); it only controls how much of this layer's effect gets through, so it can't make the effect stronger than full (C).
Q3
You selected the mask, pressed Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace to fill it black, and nothing changed. Why?
C) That shortcut fills with the background color, which was white
Why: Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace/Delete fills with the background color. Use Alt/⌥ + Backspace/Delete with black as the foreground color, or Edit ▸ Fill… ▸ Contents: Black.
Q4
While painting white to reveal the sky, you painted over part of the land by mistake. What's the best fix?
B) Press X to switch to black and paint over the mistake
Why: Masks make mistakes painless: you're only adjusting a stencil, so you can go back and forth forever. Starting over (A) wastes the work; the Eraser on the photo (C) is destructive and doesn't touch the mask at all.
Q5
An adjustment layer shows two thumbnails. Which should you click before painting, as a habit?
A) The mask thumbnail (the white/black card)
Why: On an adjustment layer the brush lands on the mask anyway (it has no pixels of its own), but the habit matters on pixel layers with masks, where the image thumbnail being selected means you paint the photo itself. Clicking the photo layer (C) is the classic way to paint on the picture by accident.
Q6
Your masked edit has a hard, obvious line at the horizon. What went wrong, and how do you fix it?
C) The brush was too hard; lower Hardness and paint the seam at low opacity
Why: Soft edges make believable masks. Right-click the canvas to reach Hardness, and blend the seam at about 30% opacity: that's "painting gray," a partial reveal.
Lab reference: Rescue the Sky (l02-washed-sky.jpg or own landscape)

A correct result looks like: sky-rescue-working.psd with two layers: the photo, and one Brightness/Contrast layer (Brightness pulled down) above it. The mask thumbnail is black over the land and white over the sky, with a soft gray band along the horizon. In sky-rescue-final.jpg the sky is noticeably deeper and cloud shapes are easier to see; the hills and tree look exactly as they did in the original.

Common wrong results and why:

  • White or black streaks painted onto the image. The photo layer was active, so the brush painted real pixels. Undo, click the adjustment layer's mask thumbnail, repaint.
  • Whole photo darkened, mask still all white. The fill step was skipped or used Ctrl/⌘ + Backspace (background color = white).
  • Sky only faintly darker, even where fully painted. Brush opacity was left at 30% from seam work, or the foreground wasn't pure white (press D).
  • A hard ridge or a dark band along the horizon. Hard brush, or the darkening spilled onto the hilltops. Soft brush at low opacity; press X and paint black to pull it back off the land.
  • Land looks right but the sky looks gray and muddy. Brightness pulled too far. The fix is the slider, not the mask: the adjustment stays editable.
Lab reference: Spotlight (l02-spotlight-object.jpg or own portrait/object)

A correct result looks like: two adjustment layers above the photo: a brightening one whose mask is black except for a white subject-shape, and a darkening one whose mask is white over the background and black over the subject. The subject reads as gently lit and the background recedes. Going further: after Ctrl/⌘ + I the white and black swap, so the brightening moves to the background (the learner should describe the flip).

Common wrong results: both masks painted over the same area (the effects cancel out), a visible "cutout" outline around the subject from a hard brush, or a single layer adjusted twice instead of two layers.

L03 · Selections That Hold Up

Q1
What's the difference between a selection and a mask?
C) A selection is a temporary request; a mask is the saved, editable answer
Why: Marching ants vanish when you deselect or click elsewhere. Converted to a mask, the same boundary is written down as white (inside) and black (outside), and you can paint on it later. B has the two backwards; the lesson FAQ calls them "cousins," not the same thing (A).
Q2
On a busy photo, Select Subject grabbed the wrong thing. What should you try?
B) Use the Object Selection tool and draw a box around exactly the thing you want
Why: Select Subject guesses the most prominent subject. The Object Selection tool lets you point: it snaps to the object inside your box. The Eraser (C) destroys pixels.
Q3
With the Quick Selection tool, how do you subtract background that got included?
A) Hold Alt/⌥ while you paint over it
Why: Paint to add, Alt/⌥-paint to subtract. Pressing Delete (C) would erase the selected pixels, the exact habit this lesson avoids.
Q4
Your subject's hair edges look chunky. Which tool rebuilds fine strands?
C) The Refine Edge Brush in Select & Mask, painted along the hairline
Why: It's built specifically to reconstruct fine strands; a normal brush can't (A). Paint along the hairline, not the solid interior.
Q5
In Select & Mask, which Output To setting keeps your cutout non-destructive in this lesson's build?
B) Layer Mask
Why: Output To: Layer Mask saves the answer as an editable mask on your photo layer. (If Decontaminate Colors is checked, Photoshop switches to New Layer with Layer Mask, which is also non-destructive: the original layer is kept, just hidden.) Deleting (A) destroys pixels.
Q6
Your cutout has a bright halo and a colored fringe from the old background. What helps?
A) Shift Edge slightly negative, plus Decontaminate Colors
Why: Shift Edge slightly negative bites off leftover background; Decontaminate Colors removes the colored fringe. A razor-sharp edge (C) is itself a tell-tale sign of a bad cutout; a believable edge is slightly soft.
Lab reference: Cut Out a Subject (l03-subject-busy-bg.jpg or own photo)

A correct result looks like: cutout-working.psd with the subject layer carrying a layer mask (white subject silhouette on black) and a calm background layer beneath it (l03-calm-background.jpg or a solid fill). No bright halo, no razor-sharp edge. Shift-clicking the mask thumbnail brings the busy background back instantly, proof nothing was deleted.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Background layer is transparent checkerboard, no mask. The learner pressed Delete on the selection. Undo (or reopen the original) and redo with Output To: Layer Mask.
  • Marching ants but no mask after OK. Output To was left on Selection.
  • Calm background covers the subject. The new layer was created above. Drag it below, or Ctrl/⌘-click the New Layer icon next time. If it won't go below, the photo is still a locked Background layer: click its lock first.
  • Thin light outline around the subject. Halo: Shift Edge slightly negative, a touch of Feather, Decontaminate Colors.
  • Bits of the busy background left inside the subject shape. Step 2 was skipped. Paint black on the mask to hide them (or Alt/⌥-subtract before refining).
Lab reference: Harder Subject (l03-fuzzy-subject.jpg onto l03-other-scene.jpg, or own hair/fur/plant photo)

A correct result looks like: the wispy fibers survive as soft, slightly translucent strands over the new background instead of being chopped into a smooth blob; little or no green fringe from the original background. A clash in color or light between subject and new background is expected and should be noted, not fixed: that's Module 4. Going further: after Select ▸ Inverse, the background is selected.

Common wrong results: a smooth, "helmet" edge (Refine Edge Brush not used, or used on the solid center); strands with a green cast (Decontaminate Colors off); strands missing because Smooth was pushed high.

L04 · Cleaning Up: Spots, Distractions & Edges

Q1
What's the difference between healing and cloning?
B) Healing blends the borrowed texture's color and brightness into its surroundings; cloning copies pixels exactly
Why: Both borrow good pixels; they differ in how they blend. Heal to blend, clone to copy. Neither is limited to skin or skies (A), and neither needs a selection (C): Content-Aware Fill is the one that does.
Q2
Why do all your cleanup on a separate, empty retouch layer?
C) The original photo is never touched, and a bad heal can simply be erased off that layer
Why: It's the Module 0 tracing-paper principle applied to retouching. The tools work on any layer (A), and the export is flattened into one image anyway, so an extra layer doesn't make the JPEG smaller (B).
Q3
You click a dust spot with the Spot Healing Brush on your empty retouch layer, and nothing happens. What's the likely cause?
A) Sample All Layers is unchecked in the options bar
Why: Without it, the tool samples only the empty layer, so there's nothing to borrow. Zooming to 100% (B) and a brush slightly larger than the spot (C) are both what the lesson recommends.
Q4
A pole stands right against a building's straight, hard roofline. Following the decision tree, which tool is best for the part along that edge?
C) Clone Stamp
Why: Hard edges, straight lines, and patterns call for copying the structure exactly. Healing tools blend with whatever's nearby and smear hard edges (A); a huge content-aware selection (B) invites smears and repeats.
Q5
On your retouch layer, the Clone Stamp paints nothing at all. What do you do?
B) Set the Clone Stamp's Sample: menu to Current & Below, then Alt/⌥-click a clean source
Why: The Clone Stamp's Sample: menu is a separate setting from the Spot Healing Brush's checkbox (A); on the default Current Layer it samples the empty layer. Cloning directly on the photo (C) breaks the non-destructive habit.
Q6
How do you know a photo is "clean enough"?
A) One pass at 100% zoom, then view it fit-to-screen: if nothing pulls your eye from the subject, you're done
Why: Straight from the lesson FAQ. Perfect at 400% that no one will ever see is wasted effort (B), and there's no need to use every tool (C).
Lab reference: Clean & Export (l04-dusty-sky.jpg or own photo)

A correct result looks like: cleanup-working.psd with the untouched photo, a retouch layer holding the spot heals, Remove-tool work, and clone strokes (toggling its eyeball brings the dust spots back), and possibly a separate Content-Aware Fill output layer. In cleanup-final.jpg, at 100%: all dust spots in the sky are gone, the bird and the pole with its wire are gone with no obvious smear or repeated shape, and the rooftop edge beside the pole is still straight and crisp.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Retouch layer is empty; the fixes are on the photo. The photo layer was active. It still looks fine, but it isn't non-destructive: redo on the retouch layer if time allows, and name it in feedback.
  • A few spots remain. The learner worked zoomed out or not methodically. Zoom to 100% and scan left to right, top to bottom.
  • Soft gray smudge where the pole was, or a wavy roofline. A healing or content-aware tool blended a hard edge. Clone Stamp along the edge.
  • A cloud or the bird appears twice. Content-aware repeat. Redo with a smaller selection, or clone out the remnant.
  • Content-Aware Fill produced nothing, or an error. The empty retouch layer was active or there was no selection. Click the photo layer, select, then run it.
Lab reference: Two Ways (l04-photobomb.jpg or own photo)

A correct result looks like: the trash can and the figure removed twice, once per method (on separate layers or in two saved versions), with a comparison filled in. There's no single right "winner": on the practice file's plain sand and sea, both usually work; a good answer names the tell-tale signs actually found (smear, repeat, halo) and what fixed them. Going further (combining L03 + L04) is optional.

Common wrong results: removing the object only once; judging at fit-to-screen instead of 100%; cloning with Current Layer sampling and concluding "the Clone Stamp doesn't work."

L05 · The Develop Step: Camera Raw (workbook self-check)

Q1
Why convert the layer to a Smart Object before choosing Filter ▸ Camera Raw Filter?
B) So Camera Raw is applied as a re-editable Smart Filter you can reopen and change any time
Why: On a Smart Object, the filter is an instruction on the container, so every slider stays live. A is a common misconception: the filter does run on a normal layer, but there it bakes in. C is false; Smart Objects usually make files a little larger.
Q2
An indoor photo has orange-looking whites. What's the fastest first move in Camera Raw?
A) Click the White Balance eyedropper on something that should be neutral gray or white
Why: The eyedropper tells Camera Raw "this should have no color," and the cast snaps away. Then fine-tune Temperature and Tint. Exposure and Contrast change brightness, not color.
Q3
Which order does the develop pass follow?
C) White Balance → Exposure → Contrast → Highlights/Shadows → Whites/Blacks
Why: The lesson works the panel top to bottom: color first, then overall brightness, then the ends of the range. Setting Whites/Blacks before Exposure means redoing them after Exposure moves everything.
Q4
What does holding Alt/⌥ while dragging the Whites or Blacks slider show you?
A) Where the image is clipping: pure white or pure black with no detail left
Why: The preview turns into a clipping map so you can push Whites until just shy of clipping and Blacks until there's a true black. A little clipping is fine (a specular reflection should be white).
Q5
What does "global before local" mean?
B) Fix the whole image first (white balance, overall tone), then make selective local tweaks
Why: Local work on a photo that still has a cast is "polishing a problem." Get the base honest first; masked, local adjustments come after (L06).
Q6
You only have a JPEG, not a RAW file. Can you still do the develop step?
C) Yes, the Camera Raw Filter runs on any layer; RAW just gives more room to recover highlights and shadows
Why: Straight from the lesson's FAQ: Filter ▸ Camera Raw Filter works on JPEG, PNG, anything. The workflow is identical; RAW files simply have more latitude.
Exercise reference: match the problem to the slider

Orange whites → White Balance eyedropper (then Temperature). Too dark overall → Exposure. Blown-out sky → Highlights down (Whites if needed). Muddy shadows → Shadows up. Flat and hazy → Contrast (and deliberate Whites/Blacks). No true black → Blacks down. Accept reasonable alternatives if the learner can explain them.

Lab notes: Develop a Flat Photo

Correct result: the layer thumbnail shows the Smart Object page badge; Camera Raw Filter is listed under "Smart Filters" beneath it. On l05-flat-cast.jpg the gray card and white wall read neutral, the sky reads blue rather than teal or orange, the darkest shadow under the roof is near black, and the white wall has detail but is bright. File saved as grade-working.psd.

Common wrong results and why:

  • No Smart Filter in the Layers panel. They skipped Convert to Smart Object, so Camera Raw baked into the pixels. Fix: undo, convert, redo.
  • Photo turned blue or magenta. The eyedropper was clicked on something that isn't neutral (the orange-lit wall, skin, the sky). Re-click on the gray card or white wall.
  • Still hazy after Contrast. Whites and Blacks weren't set. A hazy image usually has no true black; drag Blacks down with the Alt/⌥ preview.
  • Large pure-white areas in the sky. Whites or Exposure pushed too far. Pull Highlights down and back Whites off until the clipping map shows only specks.
  • Looks right on the projector, green on the laptop (or vice versa). A screen problem, not an editing problem. Judge on the learner's own screen with Night Light / True Tone off.

L06 · Curves: Precise Tone & Color (workbook self-check)

Q1
On an untouched Curves graph, what does the straight diagonal line mean?
A) Every tone leaves exactly as it came in: no change
Why: Input across, output up. On the diagonal, output equals input for every tone. The mantra: "the diagonal is leave it alone."
Q2
A photo looks flat and hazy. Which curve adds contrast?
C) An S-curve: the shadows point dragged down, the highlights point dragged up
Why: Pulling shadows darker and highlights brighter spreads the tones apart; the steeper middle is more contrast. A only brightens; B fades the blacks, which reduces contrast.
Q3
What does lifting the bottom-left corner (the black end) of the curve do?
B) Gives "milky," faded blacks for a matte or vintage look
Why: The darkest input now outputs as dark gray, so nothing reaches true black. That's the matte grade from the lesson's table.
Q4
In the Blue channel you lift the shadow end slightly and drop the highlight end slightly. What's the result?
A) Cool (blue) shadows and warm (yellow) highlights
Why: In the Blue channel, up adds blue and down adds its opposite, yellow. Cool shadows + warm highlights is the backbone of the "teal & orange" look; pull the Red channel's shadow end down slightly for truer teal (less red = more cyan).
Q5
You added a Curves layer that brightens the photo, but you want it to affect only your subject. What next?
B) Fill its mask black, then paint white over the subject with a soft brush
Why: Black hides the adjustment everywhere; white reveals it where you paint. This is the Lesson 1.1 mask skill applied to Curves ("dodging with Curves"). A weakens it everywhere; C would damage the photo layer.
Q6
Where should your Curves adjustment layers sit relative to the Camera Raw develop?
C) Above it: develop globally first, then stack Curves on top
Why: From the lesson's FAQ. Adjustment layers affect everything below them, so they need the developed base underneath. Global before local, again.
Exercise reference: sketch the curve

1 · Brighter overall: one point in the middle pushed up (a gentle hump above the diagonal). 2 · More contrast: an S: lower quarter below the diagonal, upper quarter above it. 3 · Faded, matte blacks: the bottom-left endpoint lifted straight up so the curve starts above the corner; usually paired with a gentle S.

Lab notes: Contrast & Color

Correct result: above the Camera Raw Smart Object sit two Curves layers. The first holds a gentle S-curve (and possibly small channel moves); the image has more depth but no blotchy skin or banded sky. The second is brightened and has a mostly black mask with a soft white area over one region (subject, foreground, or sky). Toggling the second layer's eye shows a subtle local lift, not a spotlight with a hard edge.

Common wrong results and why:

  • Harsh, crunchy contrast. The S is too steep or has extra points. Two points, small moves.
  • Strong color tint everywhere. Channel curve moved too far. A few pixels on Blue is already a strong grade.
  • Hard-edged glowing blob. A hard brush (or 100% opacity with a small brush) painted the mask. Use a large, soft brush; lower brush opacity if needed.
  • White smears on the photo. The image thumbnail, not the mask, was selected while painting (or they painted on the Smart Object). Undo, select the mask thumbnail.
  • The masked lift affects everything. The mask is still white. They painted white on a white mask. Fill it black first (Alt + Backspace / ⌥ + Delete with black foreground, or Ctrl/⌘ + I).
  • Curves layers under the Smart Object. The develop now sits on top of the Curves results. Drag the Curves layers above it.

L07 · Smart Objects & Finishing the Grade (workbook self-check)

Q1
Which is the best one-line definition of a Smart Object?
C) A container that stores your original pixels; every edit is a re-editable instruction
Why: This is the lesson's "one-line definition worth memorizing." Smart Objects don't enhance anything by themselves, and they usually add file size rather than reduce it.
Q2
You scale a layer to 30% and back to 100%. The normal layer turns mushy but the Smart Object stays crisp. Why?
B) The Smart Object re-renders from its sealed original pixels
Why: Scaling a normal layer down discards pixels permanently; scaling back up has to invent them. A Smart Object only changes the "display me at…" instruction and re-renders from the original each time.
Q3
You applied Smart Sharpen as a Smart Filter and want it on the eyes but not the skin. How?
A) Paint black on the Smart Filters mask over the skin
Why: Smart Filters carry a shared mask; black hides the filter, white reveals it. The lesson's own example is "sharpen the eyes but not the skin."
Q4
You want a Gaussian Blur Smart Filter to look like a soft glow, not a blurry photo. What do you change?
C) Double-click the filter's Blending Options icon; set Mode to Soft Light (or Screen) at about 20–40% opacity
Why: The filter's own blending options blend the blurred version with the sharp one. More radius only makes it blurrier; rasterizing would throw away the re-editability.
Q5
When should you not work on a Smart Object?
B) When healing or cloning directly on the layer's pixels
Why: Pixel-level tools need normal pixels. The lesson's pattern: retouch on normal layers first, then convert to a Smart Object for filtering and transforming. Module 3 depends on this.
Q6
You exported module2-grade-final.jpg. Why keep the layered .psd too?
A) The PSD is your re-editable master; the JPEG is flat and can't be re-graded
Why: The JPEG is a flattened deliverable. Every live decision (Camera Raw, Curves, Smart Filters, masks) exists only in the PSD.
Exercise reference: crisp or mushy, and "in your own words"

Crisp or mushy: the normal copy's thin lines and small text come back soft, blurry, or jagged; the Smart Object copy looks identical to the original. Own words: accept any answer that captures both halves: the original pixels are kept safe inside, and edits are changeable instructions (for example: "It's a sealed box around your photo. Anything you do is a note on the box you can change later, so the photo itself never gets damaged.").

Lab notes: Finish the Grade

Correct result: the photo layer is a Smart Object with at least two Smart Filters nested beneath it (Camera Raw plus Smart Sharpen or Gaussian Blur), the Smart Filters mask shows some black where the filter is hidden, the Curves layers above are still live, and scaling down and back up left no softness. Two files exist: the layered grade-working.psd and module2-grade-final.jpg (Export As, JPG, about 80% quality).

Fake shallow depth of field (if time): on l07-subject-busy-bg.jpg, the brick-and-dot background is soft, and the subject (and its edge) stays sharp because the filter mask is black over it.

Common wrong results and why:

L09 · Frequency Separation (workbook self-check)

Q1
In frequency separation, what does the low-frequency layer hold?
B) Color and tone: the blotches and shading
Why: Low frequency is the slow, soft stuff (the "paint"). Pores, fine hairs, and edges are high frequency (the "texture").
Q2
How much Gaussian Blur should the LOW layer get?
A) Just until the blotches are gone and the pores just disappear, no more
Why: Often around 6–12 px on a portrait, but it varies with image size, so judge by eye. Too much blur and LOW loses the face's natural shading.
Q3
For an 8-bit photo, which Apply Image settings build the HIGH layer?
C) Layer: LOW, Subtract, Scale 2, Offset 128, then set HIGH to Linear Light
Why: Subtracting the blurred copy leaves only the fine detail, centered on mid-gray; Linear Light adds it back on top of LOW. For 16-bit the recipe is Add, Scale 2, Offset 0, Invert.
Q4
Your HIGH layer is flat gray and the photo won't look normal again. What's the most likely cause?
A) HIGH's blend mode isn't set to Linear Light (or you used the wrong recipe for the bit depth)
Why: A flat gray HIGH layer is expected right after Apply Image; Linear Light is what makes it recombine. The lesson's FAQ names these two causes. The amount of blur on LOW changes the split, not whether it recombines.
Q5
A flyaway hair crosses the forehead. How do you remove it without shifting color?
B) Healing Brush or Clone Stamp on HIGH, with Sample: Current Layer
Why: The hair is texture, so it lives on HIGH. Sampling other layers (C) would drag color onto the texture sheet. Painting on LOW (A) only smears color; the hair's texture stays.
Q6
Why not just run a big Gaussian Blur on the whole face?
C) Blur smooths texture and color together, so the pores vanish and skin looks plastic
Why: That's the exact problem frequency separation solves: smooth one sheet without harming the other. The lesson's solo variation has learners compare the two side by side.
Exercise reference: recipe from memory, and HIGH-off

Recipe: Stamp Visible = Ctrl + Alt + Shift + E (Windows) / ⌘ + ⌥ + ⇧ + E (Mac) · the lower copy, LOW, is blurred · 8-bit Apply Image = Subtract / 2 / 128 · HIGH = Linear Light · blotches are evened on LOW · stray hair removed on HIGH with Sample: Current Layer. HIGH-off: with HIGH hidden, the face looks soft and blurry with no pores (pure color and shading); with it on, the texture returns on top of the evened tone.

Lab notes: Separate & Smooth

Correct result: above the earlier layers sit a blurred LOW (Normal) and a gray-looking HIGH (Linear Light) on top. Before any smoothing, the image is indistinguishable from the original. Common wrong results and why:

  • Gray mess. Linear Light not set, Apply Image Layer not LOW, or the 8-bit recipe on a 16-bit file (or vice versa). Delete HIGH and redo.
  • Image looks slightly off before any smoothing (halos, shifted contrast). Usually LOW and HIGH swapped, or Apply Image run on the wrong layer. Rebuild from the stamped layer.
  • Flat, shapeless face. LOW was blurred too hard, or the smoothing was painted across the whole face. Less blur; work only on the blotchy areas.
  • Visible outlines around smoothed areas. Lasso selection wasn't feathered. Feather 10–20 px before the small blur.
  • Plastic skin anyway. They blurred or painted on HIGH. Texture edits on HIGH should be heal/clone only.
  • Color smudge where the hair was. Healing on HIGH with Sample: All Layers (or Current & Below), which pulled color onto the texture sheet. Redo with Sample: Current Layer.

L10 · Dodge & Burn: Sculpting Light (workbook self-check)

Q1
On a 50% gray layer set to Soft Light, what does painting with white do?
B) Lightens (dodges) that area
Why: In Soft Light, lighter than 50% gray lightens and darker darkens. White = dodge, black = burn. At ~10% opacity it builds up gently rather than painting white on the face.
Q2
Why use a 50% gray layer in Soft Light mode instead of an empty Normal layer?
C) 50% gray is invisible in Soft Light, so only your light and dark strokes show, and it stays re-editable
Why: On a Normal layer, white and black paint would simply cover the face. In Soft Light, the neutral gray disappears and your strokes act as light and shadow, on their own layer.
Q3
Which brush setup matches the lesson?
A) Large, soft brush at about 10% opacity, building up with many passes
Why: "Ten percent, many passes." You can always add more, but you can't un-see a harsh streak. Small, hard, or 100% brushes are the usual cause of smudgy, fake sculpting.
Q4
In your portrait the light comes from the left. How should you dodge and burn?
B) Dodge the left-facing planes and burn the right, enhancing the existing light
Why: Follow the existing light and exaggerate it a little. Evening it out (A) flattens the face; inventing new light (C) looks wrong instantly.
Q5
How does the lesson brighten the eyes and teeth?
C) A slightly brightened Curves layer with its mask filled black, then white painted over the eye whites and teeth
Why: A masked Curves layer is non-destructive and adjustable. Keep it subtle: teeth should look clean, not glowing. Pure white paint (A) looks fake; Exposure (B) brightens everything.
Q6
Why is the gray layer the pro default over the Dodge and Burn tools?
A) The tools paint directly on pixels (destructive); the gray layer stays re-editable
Why: From the lesson's FAQ. The tools are fine on a duplicate layer for a quick fix, but the gray layer wins for real work. Both tools are still in Photoshop and work in 8-bit.

L11 · Cut It Out Cleanly

Q1
Your cutout looked perfect on its original background but looks "pasted" on a sunset. What is the most likely cause?
B) The edge pixels still carry color from the old background (a fringe)
Why: when the subject was photographed, the edge pixels picked up color from what was behind it. The original background hides that color; a new one reveals it. Resolution (A) and blend mode (C) are not the issue.
Q2
What does Decontaminate Colors do?
B) Replaces the old background's color in the edge pixels so the edge can adopt the new scene
Why: it cleans color out of the edge rather than just choosing pixels. It is the biggest single difference between an amateur and a believable cutout.
Q3
With Decontaminate Colors ticked, which Output To setting does the lesson use?
C) New Layer with Layer Mask
Why: decontamination rewrites edge pixels, so it cannot stay fully non-destructive and Photoshop forces the result onto a new layer. The mask keeps the hiding editable, and the original layer stays underneath as a backup.
Q4
Where should you paint with the Refine Edge Brush?
B) Only on soft, wispy edges like hair and fur
Why: the brush rebuilds fine strands. Dragged across a hard edge (shoulder, jaw) it turns crisp detail to mush, and it cannot invent hair the original selection never included.
Q5
What is the lesson's "believability test" for a finished cutout?
A) Drop a saturated colored layer behind it and check whether a fringe appears
Why: if the edge stays clean against a wild color, it will stay clean against any real scene. If a fringe appears, raise the decontamination Amount. Zooming out (B) hides the problem.
Q6
What is the keyboard shortcut for Select and Mask?
C) Ctrl+Alt+R / ⌘+⌥+R
Why: Ctrl/⌘+J duplicates a layer (or copies a selection to a new layer); Ctrl/⌘+Alt/⌥+G creates a clipping mask (Lesson 12). Select and Mask is also on the Select menu and the options bar of any selection tool.
Lab reference: A Composite-Ready Cutout

A correct result: a group named SUBJECT containing a new layer with a layer mask; the original photo layer is present and hidden beneath; against a saturated Solid Color fill the hair edge shows fine, soft strands and no colored halo; solid edges (shoulders, clothing) are crisp. Common wrong results and why:

You're exporting the poster for social media. Which settings are right?
A) RGB, JPG or PNG, pixel dimensions that fit the platform
Why: screens are RGB, and on screen the ppi number doesn't matter, only pixel dimensions. CMYK at 300 ppi as a PDF/X (B) is the print route.
Q5
Your printer wants 0.125 in of bleed. Photoshop has no bleed setting. How do you add it?
C) Image ▸ Canvas Size to add it on every side, then extend the background into it
Why: you build the bleed into the canvas. Keep type well inside the trim, not at the edges (B). Export As (A) has no bleed option.
Q6
After scaling a layer down, its stroke and shadow look too heavy. What fixes it?
B) Right-click the fx ▸ Scale Effects (or adjust the values by hand)
Why: transforming a layer doesn't resize its effects; they keep their pixel values. Scale Effects (also Layer ▸ Layer Style ▸ Scale Effects) resizes them to match. Image ▸ Image Size scales them only when Scale Styles is ticked.
Lab reference: Finish the Poster (Module 5 deliverable)

A correct result: the title shows an fx badge with Gradient Overlay (two tones of the accent) and a soft, low-opacity Drop Shadow that lifts it gently off the page. The badge has a Stroke and soft shadow, and the same style appears on at least one other element (pasted). The sponsor logo (l16-sponsor-logo.png or the learner's own) is a Smart Object; a hero image (l16-hero-stage.jpg or the learner's Module 4 composite) sits behind the type as a Smart Object via Place Embedded. No layer carries more than two or three effects. Margins are even; the hierarchy from L14 still reads at a squint. The layered .psd is intact and a screen JPG has been exported. (Homework print version: canvas enlarged by the bleed on every side with the background extended, type inside the trim, CMYK conversion on a copy or via the printer's PDF/X preset, saved with File ▸ Save a Copy as Photoshop PDF.)

Common wrong results and why:

  • Hard, black, far-offset shadow: default-ish values pushed up. Lower opacity, reduce distance, raise size a little.
  • Bevel, glow, stroke, and shadow on everything: effect enthusiasm. Remove until each remaining effect has a job.
  • Soft, jaggy logo: pasted as pixels and scaled up. Re-place it with File ▸ Place Embedded as a Smart Object.
  • Inconsistent badges: each styled by hand. Copy/Paste Layer Style.
  • Hero image hides the type: layer order. The photo goes at the bottom, above the background only.
  • Poster exported in CMYK for social, or colors look dull online: the print route was used for screen. Screen = RGB JPG.
  • Only a flattened file exists: the master was flattened. Reinforce: the .psd is the master, always.

L17 · Generative Fill

Q1
What is the basic Generative Fill workflow?
B) Select an area, then describe what should be there
Why: The selection is the "where" and the prompt is the "what." Photoshop reads the surrounding image for context and generates content to fit the selection. There is no generative brush, and the prompt never decides placement on its own.
Q2
You want to remove a sign from the shoreline. After selecting it, what do you do?
C) Leave the prompt empty and click Generate
Why: An empty prompt tells Photoshop to fill with plausible background. Typing "sign" would ask it to generate a sign; pressing Delete leaves a hole or flat color.
Q3
Where does a Generative Fill result go?
A) On a new generative layer with its own mask
Why: The original pixels are never touched. You can hide, mask, lower the opacity of, delete, or stack another generation on the result: it is one more non-destructive layer.
Q4
Which prompt is most likely to work well?
B) "a wooden park bench"
Why: Short and concrete wins. A vague prompt gives Photoshop nothing to build; a long stylistic one fights the scene's existing light. Name the object and let Photoshop match the lighting.
Q5
Every lighthouse variation comes out short and squat. What should you change first?
C) Make a taller selection
Why: Generative Fill fills the shape you give it, so the selection's size and proportion strongly steer the result. "Selecting the right space is half the prompt."
Lab reference: Remove & Add

Correct result: generative-working.psd with the untouched original at the bottom and at least two generative layers above it: one from an empty prompt (the distraction is gone, background continues believably) and one from a described prompt (a new element that sits in the scene's light and perspective). At least one refine move is visible: a painted mask edge, a clipped Curves or Hue/Saturation, or a healing layer. At 100% there are no obvious ghosts or smears.

Common wrong results: Halo or ghost outline where the object was: the selection hugged the object; redo with a looser selection. Blurry, mushy fill: the selection was too large; generate in smaller pieces. New element too bright or wrong color: not refined; clip an adjustment to the generative layer. Only one layer in the file, or edits on the Background: learner used Content-Aware Fill or the Remove tool on the photo itself, not Generative Fill (acceptable only under the offline fallback, and then it should be on a separate layer). Garbled hands or text: an expected limit, not a learner error; use it as a teaching moment.

Offline fallback version: removal done with the Remove tool or Content-Aware Fill on a separate layer (Sample All Layers on), no "add" step. Grade it on the same checklist minus the variation and prompt items.

L18 · Generative Expand & Reframe

Q1
What is Generative Expand, really?
A) Generative Fill aimed at empty canvas around the photo
Why: Enlarging the canvas leaves empty space, and Photoshop generates scenery to continue the photo into it. Same generative layer, same variations, same optional prompt. Nothing is stretched or upsampled.
Q2
You chose Ratio 16:9 and pressed Enter, and Photoshop cropped your photo smaller. What did you skip?
C) Dragging the crop handles outward past the photo
Why: A ratio only sets the shape. Photoshop first fits that shape inside the photo, which is a normal crop. You must drag outward until the box extends past the edges (the ratio stays locked while you drag).
Q3
One big expansion came out vague and repetitive. What is the better approach?
B) Expand in smaller steps, generating each time
Why: Huge single expansions spread the generated detail thin. Several smaller expansions stay close to real pixels and look far better. A short prompt can also give the fill something specific to build.
Q4
What is the fastest giveaway of a bad expand?
B) A horizon or strong line that jumps at the seam
Why: The eye follows lines. If the horizon runs straight through, most viewers never suspect the edges were invented. Check it first, at 100%.
Q5
What does an empty prompt do in Generative Expand?
A) Continues the existing scene into the new space
Why: Just like removal in L17, an empty prompt means "fill with what belongs here." A short prompt ("open sky," "a calm beach") steers the new area instead. Filling with the background color is what Fill: Background does, not Generative Expand.
Lab reference: Uncrop & Reframe

Correct result: expand-working.psd whose canvas is larger than the original photo, with the new margins on generative layer(s) and the original pixels intact. The subject has breathing room; the document has a useful ratio (16:9, 1:1, 4:5, or 9:16); the subject's proportions are unchanged (circles stay round). At 100%, the horizon and any strong lines run continuously across the join, and a subtle clipped Curves or unifying grade makes old and new read as one image. With l18-horizon-strip.jpg the horizon should stay dead level and the diagonal fence should continue on the same angle.

Common wrong results: Photo got smaller: ratio chosen but handles not dragged outward. Flat white or black margins: Fill was left on Background. Subject looks wider or squashed: Free Transform or Image Size was used instead of Expand. Repeated clouds, trees, or rocks in the margin: one huge leap; redo in bites. Visible step in the horizon: accepted the first variation; cycle variations or regenerate a thin strip across the seam with Generative Fill. Slight brightness or color shift at the join: skipped the unifying grade.

Offline fallback version: Crop tool dragged outward with Fill: Content-Aware Fill. Expect weaker results on large expansions; grade the workflow (outward drag, bites, seam check) rather than the fill quality.

L19 · Actions, Batch & Droplets

Q1
What is an Action?
B) A recording of steps you can replay with one click
Why: You press record, do the task once, and press stop; Photoshop remembers every step. Scripts (JavaScript) are code, a separate and more advanced tool; tool presets store settings for one tool.
Q2
Why record Fit Image instead of Image Size?
C) Fit Image fits the long edge on landscape and portrait files alike
Why: Fit Image 1200 × 1200 px makes the longer side 1200 whatever the orientation. Image Size records a fixed width, so a portrait file would come out 1200 wide and far taller than intended. Record general steps that work on any file.
Q3
Every batched file was saved into the recording folder instead of the Destination. What was missed?
A) Ticking Override Action "Save As" Commands
Why: The Action contains its own Save As step. Without the override, Batch obeys the recorded save location instead of the Destination you chose.
Q4
While recording, Save As does not offer JPEG. Which step belongs before it?
C) Layer ▸ Flatten Image
Why: After Place Embedded, the file has an extra (watermark) layer. Current Photoshop only offers JPEG in Save As for a file without extra layers, so flattening must be a recorded step before saving.
Q5
sRGB web JPGs of a whole shoot, with no recording. Which tool?
B) Image Processor
Why: File ▸ Scripts ▸ Image Processor resizes and saves a folder to JPG, PSD, or TIFF in one go, optionally running an Action. Batch needs an Action; a droplet is an Action saved as an app.
Q6
What is a droplet?
A) An Action saved as a drag-and-drop app
Why: File ▸ Automate ▸ Create Droplet saves an Action as a small app. Drag files or a folder onto it and they process; it launches Photoshop itself (which must be installed).
Lab reference: Record & Batch

Correct result: an Actions panel showing Set "Export Web" containing Action "Web JPG 1200" with these steps: Convert to Profile (sRGB), Fit Image (1200 × 1200 px), Place (the watermark), Flatten Image, Save (JPEG), Close. The l19-processed/ folder holds one JPEG per input file, each with a long edge of 1200 px (landscape 1200 wide, portrait 1200 tall, square 1200 × 1200), the watermark in a consistent position, and sRGB. The l19-input/ folder is unchanged. A droplet file exists on the desktop and processes files dropped onto it.

Common wrong results: Portrait outputs 1200 wide and ~1600 tall: Image Size was recorded instead of Fit Image. Outputs missing, or in the wrong folder: Override Action "Save As" Commands not ticked. Batch stopped after a few files: a warning dialog (missing profile) halted it; tick Suppress color profile warnings and log errors to a file. Watermark in a different spot on portrait files: position depends on canvas size; acceptable for this lab if it is fully on the canvas, a good discussion of "record on a typical image, test on an odd one." Small file enlarged to 1200 and soft (l19-batch-07.jpg): Fit Image enlarges by default; point out the Don't Enlarge option in the Fit Image dialog. Extra steps like "Select" or "Move" in the Action: the learner kept recording while exploring; delete those steps. Originals overwritten: Destination pointed at the input folder; restore from the practice folder and discuss why copies matter.

L20 · Choose Your Brief & Plan

Q1
Which three questions should you answer before opening Photoshop?
A) What is it for? Who is it for? What does "done" look like?
Why: Purpose sets every later decision; the audience shapes tone and the destination sets size, resolution, and color mode; defining "done" up front tells you when to stop. Tools, fonts, and layer counts are downstream choices.
Q2
Which of these is a complete one-line brief?
B) "A gig poster for a local band, to be seen printed on a wall, that feels loud, retro, and warm."
Why: It fills every slot of the template: what (gig poster), who (a local band's audience), where (printed on a wall), and three adjectives. A is a vague wish; C is document settings, not a brief.
Q3
What belongs on a quick moodboard?
A) A palette of 3 to 5 colors, one or two fonts, and a rough layout sketch
Why: The moodboard's only job is to make you decide mood, palette, and layout before the canvas does. Rough is fine; scribbles count.
Q4
Your piece is a poster to be printed. How do you set up the document?
B) The Print preset at real-world size, 300 ppi, converting to CMYK for the printer at export
Why: Print needs real-world size at 300 ppi. The course designs in RGB and converts to the printer's CMYK profile at export (Module 5). A 72 ppi web document would print soft; "fix it later" means upscaling or rebuilding.
Q5
How ambitious should your capstone be?
B) One notch beyond what you have done, not ten
Why: A finished, polished piece at 80% of your dream idea beats an abandoned masterpiece. Repeating something already made teaches little; the epic version can come next.
Reference: a solid plan

Looks like: a one-line brief with all four slots filled; a clear definition of "done"; 5 to 10 references with a note on what each contributes; 3 to 5 palette colors; one or two fonts; a thumbnail layout that shows the focal point, title, and supporting info; a skill map of specific moves tagged by module (e.g. "cut out subject (M4), match light (M4), type hierarchy (M5), grade (M2)"); and a document whose size, resolution, and color mode match the destination. The partner's answer to "What do you expect this to look like?" roughly matches the brief.

Common weak plans: Adjectives missing or generic ("nice, cool, good"): nothing to steer decisions; ask "how should a viewer feel?" Three fonts or eight colors: Module 5's rule is one or two fonts; trim. Skill map says "Photoshop stuff": ask for moves, not topics. Scope for a series (three posters, a full brand system): scale to one piece plus, at most, one variation. Web-sized document for a print piece: rebuild the document now, before any work goes into it.

L21 · Studio Session: Build It Well

Q1
What is the right build order for a big piece?
B) Block in the composition roughly, then refine big to small
Why: Time spent perfecting an eyelash before the composition is decided is time you will throw away. Only once the big shapes work do details deserve attention.
Q2
How do you give a layer group a color label?
A) Right-click the group's eye (visibility) icon and pick a color
Why: That is the lesson's method. Renaming is done by double-clicking the group name; double-clicking a thumbnail does something else (for example, opens a Smart Object's contents).
Q3
Which statement about History snapshots is true?
B) History clears when you close the file, snapshots included, so you should also save versioned files
Why: Snapshots are session-only restore points. Versioned saves (capstone_v1.psd, _v2) protect you across sessions and crashes.
Q4
What does View ▸ Flip Horizontal do?
B) Flips only the view, so you see the piece with fresh eyes
Why: It changes only how the canvas is displayed, so there is nothing to undo; choose it again to flip back. Fresh eyes catch imbalances familiar eyes miss.
Q5
Your capstone file is huge and slow. What is the safe first move?
A) Save a full layered version, then merge or flatten finished sections in a working copy
Why: You keep an editable master and gain speed in the copy. Flattening the master destroys editability. Converting groups to Smart Objects tidies the stack but usually does not shrink the file, because the layers are kept inside. Edit ▸ Purge also helps if history is eating memory.
Reference: a well-built capstone file

Looks like: a capstone_final.psd master plus at least one earlier version on disk; top-level groups named by role (BACKGROUND, SUBJECT, TYPE, GRADE or similar) and color-labeled; no "Layer 12 copy 3"; adjustments on adjustment layers, placed photos as Smart Objects, and edits controlled by masks; a unifying grade at the top of the stack. The image meets the strong-piece rubric: a clear focal point, consistent light and color, clean edges and masks at 100%, deliberate type (if any), and no distracting artifacts.

Brief-specific checks: Poster: one dominant title, clear hierarchy, type inside the trim. Surreal composite: shadows and light direction agree; grain and color match across elements. Retouched portrait: skin texture kept (heal, don't erase), no plastic look, dodge & burn subtle. Restoration: scratches and dust gone without smudged detail; tone and color balanced; the result still looks like the original era. Product hero shot: clean cutout, a believable contact shadow, a background that supports rather than competes. Brand mini-kit: a logo lockup built from shapes and type; social pieces share fonts, palette, and layout via Smart Objects.

Common weak results: Flat, unnamed stack: organized "later" (never). Painted-in edits on the photo layer: destructive habits returned under pressure; ask them to rebuild the one key edit non-destructively. Halo or fringe around a cutout: skipped the 100% pass. Two focal points competing: no step-back; squint test reveals it. Elements with different light: no match or unifying grade. Over-scoped and half-finished: agree a minimum presentable version and hide unfinished elements.

L22 · Present & Ship

Q1
Your piece will be shown on screens. What do you deliver?
B) A JPG or PNG in sRGB, keeping the layered .psd master safe
Why: Screens need a flat file in sRGB (PNG if transparency is needed). The .psd is the editable master, never the delivery. A CMYK PDF with bleed is the print delivery, to the printer's spec.
Q2
How do you put your art into a mockup template?
A) Double-click the Smart Object layer's thumbnail, place your art in the .psb tab, save, and close it
Why: Mockups are built around a Smart Object. Editing its contents and saving updates the mockup, which warps the art to fit the scene. Pasting on top loses the warp and lighting; flattening destroys the template.
Q3
What goes in the two-sentence artist statement?
B) What it is and who it is for, then one interesting decision you made and why
Why: Sentence one orients the viewer; sentence two shows your thinking. Tool lists bore viewers, and apologies undercut the work before anyone has seen it.
Q4
The rubric's "Believability" row asks…
A) Do shadows agree on one light? Any telltale AI artifacts?
Why: Believability is about whether the image could be real. Font count belongs to the Type row; file size is not a rubric item.
Q5
"Done is a decision" means…
B) Use the rubric to decide the piece is finished, then ship it
Why: No piece is ever perfect. The rubric lets you call it finished with confidence; shipping a good piece today beats polishing a slightly better one forever.
Reference: a shipped capstone

Looks like: a delivery file named clearly and sized for its destination (sRGB JPG/PNG for screen; for print, a 300 ppi CMYK PDF with bleed to the printer's spec, plus a screen JPG for the class projector); the art shown in at least one context mockup with a soft shadow; a before/after or process image; a two-sentence statement in the "what and who, then one decision and why" pattern; the layered master saved separately. Generative content is disclosed where honesty matters.

Common weak results: Delivered the .psd or a CMYK JPG for screen (colors look off): re-export in sRGB. Art pasted flat over the mockup: did not edit the Smart Object, so no warp or lighting. Mockup without a shadow: looks pasted on. Statement that lists tools or apologizes: rewrite in the two-sentence pattern. Rubric self-score all "strong" with visible halos: ask them to zoom to 100% on the edges row.

Critique quality: what good peer feedback sounds like

Strong: "The first thing I notice is the red title. What confused me was the second figure on the left. On Light & color: the warm grade really sells the summer-night feel (glow). I wonder if the figure's shadow could fall to the right like the others (grow)." Weak: "I love it." (not specific) · "I'd have made it blue." (taste, not the brief) · "The whole thing doesn't work." (not actionable). Coach weak feedback toward a rubric row and a pointable spot on screen.
