Module 6 • Lesson 1🪄 Generative Fill

Welcome to the Advanced tier. Generative Fill lets you add or remove parts of an image by describing them in plain words, and Photoshop paints the result onto a new layer. It feels like magic, but it is a tool with rules, strengths, and blind spots. This lesson teaches you to drive it well.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Remove an object by selecting it and generating with an empty prompt
  • Add new content by selecting an area and describing what belongs there
  • Work with generative layers and cycle through variations
  • Write prompts that work, and know Generative Fill's limits

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50 minutes

🎯 Project: Take one photo and both remove a distraction and add a believable new element, non-destructively.

✨ The Goal: Describe It Into Being

Same lake, two edits at once. On the right, an ugly sign clutters the shore and the water is empty. On the left, the sign is gone (removed with an empty prompt) and a sailboat has appeared (added with the prompt "a small sailboat"). Drag the handle:

🧠 Mental Model: Select, Then Describe

Generative Fill always works the same way, and it is worth saying out loud because it makes everything predictable: you select an area, then you describe what should be there. Photoshop looks at the surrounding image for context, generates content to fit, and places it on a brand-new generative layer with its own mask. Your original pixels are never touched.

Two everyday jobs come straight out of that one behavior:

  • Remove: select an object and generate with an empty prompt. Photoshop fills the selection with plausible background, a smarter cousin of Content-Aware Fill from Module 1.
  • Add: select an empty area and describe what to put there. Photoshop generates it in the scene's light and perspective.

🧠 It is a layer, not a commitment

Because the result lands on a generative layer, everything you know applies: hide it, mask it, lower its opacity, delete it, or stack another generation on top. Generative Fill is not a one-way button. It is one more non-destructive layer, the principle from Module 0 all over again.

🛠️ Guided Build: Remove & Add

Open a photo with a clear distraction and some open space. Save as generative-working.psd. (Generative Fill needs a recent Photoshop, an internet connection, and an Adobe account; it spends generative credits.)

Step 1: Remove a distraction · 8 min

  1. Select the object with any selection tool. A loose selection with a little margin works best.
  2. In the task bar, click Generative Fill, leave the prompt empty, and click Generate.
  3. Photoshop fills the selection with background on a new generative layer. Cycle the variations and pick the cleanest.

Step 2: Add a new element · 8 min

  1. Select an empty area where something could believably go (open water, sky, a tabletop).
  2. Click Generative Fill in the task bar and type a short, concrete prompt: a small sailboat, a coffee cup, storm clouds. Click Generate.
  3. Review the three variations. Keep one, or click Generate again for three more.

✅ Match the selection to the subject

Want a tall element? Make a tall selection. Generative Fill fills the shape you give it, so the selection's size and proportion strongly steer the result. Selecting the right space is half the prompt.

Step 3: Blend and refine · 6 min

  1. Because it is a normal layer with a mask, refine it with everything you know: mask a rough edge (Module 1), match its light and color with clipped adjustments (Module 4), heal a small artifact (Module 3).
  2. Generative results are a strong starting point, not always a finished one. Your craft finishes the job.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ A distraction is removed via an empty-prompt generation
  • ☐ A new element is added via a described generation
  • ☐ Each result sits on its own generative layer (original untouched)
  • ☐ I cycled variations and picked the best
  • ☐ I refined the result with masks or adjustments as needed

⚖️ Prompts, Limits & Ethics

Prompts that work

  • Be concrete and short. "a wooden park bench" beats "something nice to sit on."
  • Describe the thing, not the style. The scene's existing light usually wins, so name the object and let Photoshop match the lighting.
  • Iterate. If three variations miss, tweak one or two words and generate again rather than rewriting from scratch.

⚠️ Know the limits

  • Fine detail (hands, text, faces at small size) can come out wrong. Zoom in and check.
  • Very large or very specific generations are less reliable than small, plausible ones.
  • It needs internet and credits, and results vary run to run.

💡 A note on honesty

Generative edits can change what a photo says, not just how it looks. For journalism, evidence, or documentary work, adding or removing content crosses a line. Adobe embeds Content Credentials to flag AI edits for exactly this reason. Use the power thoughtfully, and be transparent when it matters.

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. Remove three different distractions from one busy photo, one selection at a time.
  2. Add an element and then change your mind: regenerate with a different prompt on the same selection. Feel how cheap iteration is.
  3. Try a deliberately hard prompt (a person's hands, readable text) and observe where Generative Fill struggles. Knowing its weak spots makes you a better driver.

Going further: generate an element, then treat it as a real subject: mask it, grade it, and add a shadow with your Module 4 skills. Generative plus craft beats generative alone every time.

🍳 Recipe Card: Generative Fill

Add or remove by describing

  1. Select the area (loose, with margin), then click Generative Fill in the task bar
  2. Remove: empty prompt → Generate
  3. Add: short concrete prompt → Generate
  4. Cycle the three variations; regenerate if needed
  5. Refine on its generative layer (mask, match, heal)

Mantra: select the where, describe the what, then finish with craft.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • Generative Fill = select an area, then describe what belongs there.
  • Empty prompt removes; a described prompt adds.
  • Results land on a generative layer, fully non-destructive and refinable.
  • Prompts should be short and concrete; know the limits and edit ethically.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now add and remove content by describing it, and just as importantly, you treat the result as a layer to refine rather than a magic final answer. That combination, generative power plus real editing craft, is exactly what separates a thoughtful editor from someone mashing a button.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Generative Fill is grayed out. Why?

You likely need a current version of Photoshop, an internet connection, and to be signed in to your Adobe account. The feature runs in the cloud and uses generative credits.

My generation looks blurry or low-detail.

Very large selections spread the generated detail thin. Generate in smaller pieces, or generate then sharpen and refine. Also try regenerating; quality varies between runs.

Can I use Generative Fill results commercially?

By default Generative Fill uses Adobe's Firefly models, which Adobe designed to be commercially safe. Recent versions may also let you pick third-party (partner) models in the task bar; those come with their own terms and usually cost more credits, so check which model you used. Always check the current terms for your plan and region, and disclose AI edits where honesty matters.
