Module 4 • Lesson 1🧩 Cut It Out Cleanly

Compositing means combining two images into one that looks like a single photograph. Everything rides on the cutout, because the new background reveals every flaw the old one hid. This lesson gets your subject out cleanly, with an edge that survives being dropped anywhere.

📚 What You'll Learn

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Cut a subject out with Select & Mask, tuned for compositing
  • Rescue fine hair with the Refine Edge Brush
  • Kill the tell-tale fringe with Decontaminate Colors
  • Output the result as a layer with a mask, ready to drop into a scene

⏱️ Estimated Time: 50 minutes

🎯 Project: Produce a clean, fringe-free cutout of a subject, ready to place into a new background.

✂️ The Goal: An Edge That Survives

🧠 Mental Model: The Background Tells On You

When your subject was photographed, the edge pixels picked up color from whatever was behind them. Shoot someone against green grass and their hair edges hold a faint green. Move them onto a sunset and that green fringe screams "pasted." The old background literally clings to the border.

So a composite cutout has a harder job than a simple mask. It is not enough to choose the right pixels. You also have to clean the color out of the edge so it can adopt the new scene. That is what Decontaminate Colors does, and it is the single biggest difference between an amateur and a believable cutout.

🧠 Spiraling up from Module 1

You already made selections and masks in Lesson 1.2. This is the same skill aimed at a harder target: an edge that has to hold up against a brand-new background instead of its original one. Same tools, higher stakes.

🛠️ Guided Build: A Composite-Ready Cutout

Pick a subject shot against a plain-ish background (a portrait, a product, a pet). Save as composite-working.psd.

Step 1: Select, then enter Select & Mask · 6 min

  1. Select ▸ Subject for the first pass, then click Select and Mask….
  2. Set the View to On Layers or Overlay so you can judge the edge honestly.

Step 2: Rescue the hair · 10 min

  1. Take the Refine Edge Brush and paint along wispy hair or fur. Photoshop rebuilds the fine strands.
  2. Nudge Smooth and Feather a little, and set Shift Edge slightly negative to bite off leftover background.

⚠️ Don't over-brush

The Refine Edge Brush is for soft, wispy edges only. Drag it across a hard edge (a shoulder, a jaw) and it turns crisp detail to mush. Use it on hair, leave the solid edges alone.

Step 3: Decontaminate the fringe · 5 min

  1. Open Output Settings and tick Decontaminate Colors.
  2. Raise Amount until the colored halo at the edge disappears. Watch the hair edges especially; that is where the old background hides.
  3. Set Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask and click OK.

✅ The believability test

Temporarily drop a saturated colored layer behind your cutout. If the edge stays clean against a wild color, it will stay clean against any real scene. If a fringe appears, raise the decontamination Amount.

Step 4: Bank a clean copy · 3 min

  1. Group the cutout layer and its mask, name it SUBJECT, and keep your original photo layer hidden beneath as a backup.
  2. Save. You now have a portable, fringe-free subject ready for Lesson 4.2.

✅ Project Completion Checklist

  • ☐ Subject is on its own layer with a mask (background hidden, not deleted)
  • ☐ Hair edges look soft and natural, not chopped
  • ☐ No colored fringe survives against a saturated test background
  • ☐ The original layer is kept as a hidden backup
  • ☐ The cutout group is named and saved

🧗 Now You: Solo Variation

🌟 Your challenge

  1. Cut out a subject that was shot against a strongly colored background (green foliage, a blue wall). The fringe will be obvious, which makes decontamination easy to see working.
  2. Test the cutout against three different colored backgrounds. Fix any edge that betrays the original.
  3. Cut out something with a tricky edge next: curly hair, fur, or a glass object.

Going further: zoom to 200% and inspect the hair edge pixel by pixel. Learning to see a bad edge is half of learning to fix one.

🍳 Recipe Card: Clean Cutout

Composite-ready in five moves

  1. Select ▸ Subject, then Select and Mask…
  2. Refine Edge Brush on hair; nudge Smooth / Feather / Shift Edge
  3. Output Settings: tick Decontaminate Colors, raise Amount
  4. Output To: New Layer with Layer Mask
  5. Test against a saturated color; keep the original as a backup

Mantra: the new background reveals every sin, so clean the edge first.

📝 Lesson Summary

🎓 Key Takeaways

  • A composite cutout must survive a new background, which reveals every edge flaw.
  • Edge pixels carry color from the old background; that is the fringe.
  • Decontaminate Colors strips that fringe so the edge adopts the new scene.
  • Output to a new layer with a mask and keep the original as a backup.

🎉 What You've Accomplished

You can now produce a cutout that holds up anywhere, not just against the background it was born on. That fringe-free edge is the foundation the rest of Module 4 builds on. A great composite with a bad edge is still a bad composite, and yours is now clean.

❓ Common Questions at This Stage

Why can't decontamination stay non-destructive like a mask?

Because it actually rewrites the color of the edge pixels rather than just hiding them. That is why Photoshop forces it onto a new layer. Keep your original underneath and you still lose nothing.

My hair still looks chunky after refining.

Paint the hairline with the Refine Edge Brush specifically, not a normal brush, and make sure your original selection already included most of the hair mass. The brush rebuilds strands at the edge; it cannot invent hair that was never selected.

Should I always decontaminate?

For compositing onto a different-colored scene, yes. If you are keeping a subject on a similar background, a plain mask is often enough. Match the effort to the job.

🔭 Looking Ahead

A clean cutout is only step one. Drop it into a scene right now and it will still look pasted, because its brightness and color do not match. Lesson 4.2 makes it belong, matching light and color so the subject looks photographed in place.
